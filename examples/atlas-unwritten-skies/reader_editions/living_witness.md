# The Atlas of Unwritten Skies

*Complete story path: Living Witness*

## Prologue

The last map in the world had a hole in it. Not a tear: torn paper left fibers, a visible raggedness where the knife had passed. The Atlas of Unwritten Skies was intact.

Its vellum shone like a shallow pond at sunrise, its coastlines remained fine as hairs, its copper letters had not dimmed in three hundred years.

But where the city of Bellwether should have stood, someone had rubbed away the fact that a city had ever existed.

Mira Vale had known Bellwether in the ordinary way people know places they hope to return to.

She remembered wet pavement and a window full of pears, an argument about whether the sea was green or blue, and her father's hand holding hers so she would not cross when the tram bell sounded.

Now there was a patch of cream-colored paper in her father's atlas. She could remember him. She could no longer remember the sound of his voice.

The omission grew by one narrow street whenever the observatory clock struck twelve. Mira had taken to avoiding clocks. It had not helped.

**Mira:** There was a bakery here. A blue door and a woman who insisted that the best bread was always sold out. Her husband played the violin badly, and everyone loved him for it.

She touched the blank place with the bone tip of a measuring instrument. The ink beside her fingertip shivered. A dot of coastline slipped away, leaving the harbor suspended without water.

She had begun counting the losses in a notebook, because the losses did not count themselves.

The observatory stood on an arch of stone above a country of clouds. Its libraries occupied twelve stories; its lenses, suspended in revolving brass, were larger than rooms.

Every evening the clouds below reflected the colors of a sunrise that would not occur for another ten hours. Some called this an optical marvel. Mira called it an intolerable habit of the sky, which ought to keep a more straightforward schedule.

In her ledger she had written: Seven bridges. Two hospitals. An orchard. The names of four hundred and seventeen people.

For the last three nights she had written the same sentence underneath the list: I must find out who is doing this before the list learns to include me.

A small ember of blue light escaped the great celestial sphere above her. It drifted between brass rings, hesitated over the ledger, and settled on the drawing of the vanished harbor. Mira reached for it.

Instead of heat, she felt the cold pressure of an unfamiliar memory: a boy laughing in a stairwell, a lantern breaking in a fountain, and a name spoken too softly to hear.

The pressure faded. The blue spark remained, trapped beneath her fingertip like the reflection of a tiny star.

**Mira:** Who are you supposed to be?

The west door rang, which was alarming. No one came to the west door.

For thirty years it had opened onto a staircase that was not attached to a building, and people who attempted the stairs usually found themselves on a rooftop they had never visited.

The door rang again with the persistence of somebody who had been told to wait and had decided waiting was merely an inefficient way of knocking.

Mira closed the atlas. She did not extinguish the lamp. There are ways to make a room seem unoccupied and ways to make it seem dangerous. She preferred the second.

## Courier

The man outside wore the weather of three separate altitudes. His navy cloak had been soaked by rain and dried by wind; its hem had acquired a white rim of frost. A leather satchel rested against his hip.

His boots were old but carefully repaired, and a telescope hung from a strap beside a pocket watch without hands. He looked at Mira's expression, then at the iron hook in her hand.

**Cael:** I have a delivery, but I can come back when the room is less committed to self-defense.

**Mira:** The post office stopped delivering to the observatory eleven years ago.

**Cael:** The post office stopped existing eleven years ago. Not quite the same thing.

The man offered an envelope without stepping over the threshold. Across it, in a script Mira knew better than her own, was a sentence that made the entire building seem to tilt: TO THE DAUGHTER I WILL NO LONGER REMEMBER.

She stared at it so long that the courier slowly lowered his arm. He did not joke again.

**Mira:** Where did you find this?

**Cael:** In Bellwether. Under the south platform, where the tracks ended in the sea. I have been carrying it for eight days. Each morning I woke with fewer memories of the place I had collected it.

**Cael:** By yesterday I was not sure which direction the platform faced. Today I cannot remember the station at all, only the letter. I thought I had gone mad until I saw the hole in the map from your balcony.

**Mira:** You can see it from there?

**Cael:** There are lights under the cloudline. Not stars; stars would have the good sense to remain where they were put. Your observatory has a blue wound in its roof and all the birds avoid it.

Mira had never asked a stranger to describe the observatory from outside. She had never considered that its emptiness might be visible. The idea made her feel suddenly as if she had been living with a wound without noticing the blood on her clothes.

He said his name was Cael Vey. He had once worked the sky routes that threaded islands into the upper air. His company had gone bankrupt when the northern winds changed. A week later the company had not existed.

His colleagues vanished from the records, although their signatures remained on his old pay slips. He kept the slips folded in oilskin, not for their monetary value, but to have the signatures of people who had once addressed him by name.

**Cael:** I used to think maps were promises that the world would keep its appointments. Now I think the world has been refusing to attend for some time.

**Mira:** The world is not refusing. Someone is cancelling the appointments.

Cael considered her for a moment, as though deciding whether she was dangerous because she knew something or because she knew too little. Then he held out the envelope again.

**Cael:** In that case, I would prefer not to be late.

Mira recognized the seal. An eight-pointed compass star impressed in dark blue wax: her father's private mark, used when he wanted to tell her something the Council would never allow him to publish.

She had last seen it seventeen years ago, when he disappeared into the storm beyond the western observatory.

The paper trembled in her hands. A strange delicacy was required to handle a letter from the dead. She wanted to tear it open with her teeth.

She also wanted to put it back in its envelope and live forever in the moment before knowing what it contained.

## Courier Questions

Mira left the letter sealed and made tea for the stranger. The kettle had a cracked lid and a spout shaped like an impatient bird; it had been passed from one assistant astronomer to another for two centuries.

The ritual was not kindness so much as a way of giving her hands something steady to do.

Cael told her about the south platform, the strange wind that had blown upward through the stairs, and a woman on the station clock who had asked him to carry a letter to the place where tomorrow was being kept in jars.

He had laughed at the instruction then. He did not laugh now.

**Cael:** She said the names we lose don't disappear. They become weather. I thought she was making a joke about my profession.

**Mira:** She might have been warning you that a memory has weight even after you stop carrying it.

At last Mira opened the letter. It contained her father's confession and the warning in her own handwriting: DO NOT LET THEM OPEN THE EASTERN LENS.

Hearing the account first did not make the confession easier; it gave her one more person who might help carry the burden of it.

## Atlas Wakes

The atlas opened of its own accord. Its brass clasps lifted like hands recoiling from hot iron. Across the blank region where Bellwether belonged, pale lettering gathered and dissolved: a street, a garden, the beginning of a person's surname.

The ink never completed a word. Each attempt left a soft sound in the room, like a match being struck in water.

**Cael:** Is that normal?

**Mira:** Until this month the book required someone to turn its pages. My definition of normal has been changing.

The blue spark she had trapped beneath her finger emerged from the paper. It floated toward the suspended globe, and the globe answered with a second spark, then a third. Six of them arranged themselves into a constellation Mira had never seen.

She knew every constellation in the sky. She had also, until very recently, known every one in the atlas.

From the eastern wing came the sound of a door opening under enormous pressure. It was followed by a woman's voice, thin and distant, reciting the longitude of a city that no longer existed. Mira knew the voice.

She had learned to name the stars by following its rhythm from one line to the next. It belonged to her mother, who had died the night her father disappeared.

**Mira:** That voice has been buried for seventeen years.

**Cael:** Then perhaps the grave isn't where we think it is.

Mira closed her eyes. She saw her mother in the kitchen, sprinkling salt on a window ledge to make the weather change. She could not remember whether the salt had worked. She had forgotten the answer long before the city began vanishing.

She wondered how much of a life could be removed without leaving a blank space anyone would think to investigate.

The Council of Cartographers had forbidden the eastern wing after the catastrophe in which her parents disappeared.

Their proclamation was written in language that looked impressive and meant nothing: for the continuity of public navigation, certain navigational mechanisms are to remain undisturbed until such time as they cease to pose a navigational concern. Mira had been seven.

She could now translate the proclamation: we are afraid, and we would rather you be afraid of our authority than of what frightens us.

**Cael:** There are two ways to get to the east wing. An official staircase guarded by people who collect salaries, and the old maintenance route above the orrery. I have experience with roofs.

**Mira:** The atlas has a third path. It connects places by memory instead of distance. The last time I used it, I ended up somewhere I did not recognize and spent three days forgetting why I had gone.

**Cael:** And the safest route?

**Mira:** Sit here until Bellwether becomes a polite gap in somebody else's story.

They both looked down at the book. A small golden circle formed where the southern harbor should have been. As it widened, Mira saw fragments of a street beneath its surface. There was a boy with a red scarf.

There was a dog sleeping in sunlight. There was a shadow on a wall shaped like a woman holding an open umbrella.

They had an evening, perhaps two, before the erased region reached the observatory itself. Mira recognized the way the map pulsed: each loss was a rehearsal for a greater one. The unseen hand would keep drawing circles until no name remained to contradict it.

*Choice: memory route*

## Memory Route

Mira put her palm on the golden circle. The atlas felt impossibly warm, as if the city beneath the paper had been holding its breath for someone to return. Cael placed his hand beside hers. For a moment the observatory was still around them.

Then the sky turned inside out.

They stood on a Bellwether street paved with wet blue stone. Carts moved between lamplights. A child shouted about an escaped balloon. Nobody turned toward Mira.

At the end of the road she saw the bakery's blue door and, behind it, her father kneeling to tie her shoe. He was younger than she remembered and more tired. His face was in focus.

When he opened his mouth, she heard only wind.

**Cael:** We can see them, but we can't speak to them.

**Mira:** Perhaps the past does not owe us an answer.

A woman made of rain stepped out from the crowd. Her features were indistinct, and whenever Mira tried to remember them the street shifted to another afternoon. She held up a card with a precise instruction: A NAME IS NOT A PLACE.

THE PERSON WHO REMEMBERS IT IS. A moment later the card became a railway ticket to a station that had been erased.

Mira took the ticket. The street folded along the seam of its railway line. They returned to the observatory with water on their boots and the smell of pears in their sleeves.

The atlas had surrendered a fragment of Bellwether, but Mira's memory of her father's face was suddenly clearer. It was not healing; it was evidence that forgetting could be reversed.

## Second Chapter

By midnight the observatory had acquired a second weather. Outside, thunder rolled along the cloud shelf. Inside, a pale fog gathered on the polished marble and retreated whenever Mira spoke a name that the atlas still recognized.

The six blue sparks circled the globe in the pattern of a sentence she had not learned to read.

She spread everything they had gathered across the main table: the letter from her father, the maintenance ledger or the ticket or the account of the woman in the red coat, and the list of streets she had spent weeks writing out of fear.

Cael sat opposite her with his useless watch between his hands. He had taken its mechanisms apart years ago, he explained, because a watch without hands at least could not accuse him of being late.

**Mira:** Every route gives the same answer in a different language. Somebody made the erasure. The places haven't entirely gone. And the person who kept the machine working knew they were causing harm.

**Cael:** It may be worse. They may have convinced themselves the harm was mercy.

The atlas opened to a new page and displayed an image of the world as it had existed before the northern catastrophe. A coast ran into the distance where no coast could now be seen.

Hundreds of islands hung like scattered petals above the water. A city larger than Bellwether occupied the center of the chart. Beside it stood the word ASTERVALE, written in the old script and surrounded by twelve compass roses.

Mira had heard the name only in a children's song, in which it rhymed with 'never fail.' There were no songs in the library that actually mentioned it.

She opened the index. Someone had sewn the A pages shut. She worked the thread loose with a knife.

Beneath it she found a letter her father had never sent to the Council: What we call an empty sky is a city we have chosen not to remember. AsterVale did not fall. It was exchanged.

If the exchange is reversed without settling the debt, the breach will reopen and all that stands above the clouds will descend into it.

**Mira:** He is describing an agreement. With whom?

**Cael:** The sky, perhaps. It is full of people who insist on negotiating with the weather.

They searched the next pages. The account was not a natural history but the minutes of a council meeting. Seventeen years earlier, when the celestial engine had failed, the astronomers learned that a white storm would consume the island nations.

An ancient instrument could prevent the storm by removing its path from the map of the world. Cities, once excluded from the storm's course, would survive as unremembered territories suspended in an invisible region beside the ordinary sky.

**Cael:** They called it a rescue.

**Mira:** A city cannot consent to a rescue in which it is not allowed to exist.

But the account did not end there. The removal had saved those cities, in a limited sense. The storm had passed over the blank places without striking them. The people inside were still alive, according to the last message received through the eastern lens.

They had no memory of the world outside, and the outside world had no memory of them. They had survived by agreeing to be unfindable.

**Mira:** Why is Bellwether being erased now? The storm passed long ago.

A name flashed across the page: THE TOLL. The instrument had continued to take one memory from the outer world each year as payment for holding the other cities safe. The payment grew.

It had begun with a child's forgotten face, a lost song, a name no longer attached to a street. Now it demanded cities.

When the book reached its own maker's name, the debt would close and everything the instrument held apart would collapse into one another.

The clock struck twelve. Mira heard the first glass pane in the western corridor fracture. From the atlas came the sound of a thousand small bells ringing under water. A line of coastline disappeared.

Above the cloudline, the stars were briefly arranged into the shape of a human hand.

**Cael:** How long?

**Mira:** Until dawn. Perhaps less. My father left a warning about the eastern lens, but the only way to reach AsterVale may be to open it.

Cael picked up the atlas as if it might bite him. It was heavier than the letter. His mouth tightened in a way Mira recognized from people who had decided to say something painful before they could change their minds.

**Cael:** I think I've been there. Not in my current life. In the part of my life that keeps being edited. I know the shape of that bridge. I know where the bakery stands.

**Cael:** And every time I try to remember who took me across it, I lose the beginning of a different memory.

**Mira:** Did you carry letters between the outside and the hidden cities?

**Cael:** I carried messages people were told could not be delivered. I was very good at it. I think someone made me forget the recipients so that I would keep delivering.

Mira wanted to insist he was mistaken. Instead she asked him to show her his palms. On the inside of his right wrist was the faint scar of an eight-pointed compass rose, the same mark her father had used on his letters.

It was not tattooed. It had been burned into the skin by something that had held him against an iron plate.

The first time she had met him, his name was the one thing she had trusted. Now it felt like a bridge that might have been built from both shores by different people.

## Examine Scar

The scar was a map in miniature. Under the observatory's longest inspection glass it resolved into a system of lines too fine for an ordinary burn: channels, bridges, twelve tiny compass stars, and a black dot where AsterVale ought to have been.

A miniature of the eastern lens was written into his skin.

**Mira:** This is an access key. Your body remembers where the book won't.

**Cael:** That would explain why I never liked watches. Every watchmaker I visited insisted my pulse was out of time.

She measured the scar against the lens diagrams. Twelve concentric circles matched exactly. The key required a living messenger, not a brass mechanism, to cross from the outer world into the hidden one. Her father had known someone would be needed.

Whether he had chosen Cael or tried to prevent the choice, Mira could not yet tell.

He held still while she recorded the lines. He did not ask her to apologize for treating him as evidence. When she finished, she wrote his name on a separate page and folded it into his hand.

**Mira:** Whatever else this mark means, this is your name. I won't allow a machine to persuade you that you don't own it.

## Third Chapter

The forbidden corridor smelled of old rain and electrical dust.

Once, every room in this wing had contained a practical object: an instrument to measure the tilt of a planet, a clock that could mark the passing of clouds, a map that answered questions about roads.

Now each door was marked with a star, and each star had been scratched out. The absence of the stars unsettled Mira more than the locks. Someone had taken care not to leave a symbol by which the rooms might be remembered.

They passed a gallery of bronze busts, each covered by a dark cloth. Mira lifted the corner of one. The face beneath had been worn smooth, as if by centuries of weather, although the sculptor's date was only twenty-three years ago.

A plaque at the base read: FIRST DIRECTOR OF THE HELD CITY PROJECT. The director's name was gone.

**Cael:** It is possible to build a monument without understanding whom you are honoring.

**Mira:** That is what most monuments are.

The corridor narrowed into a passage made of glass. Clouds pressed against the walls.

Their reflections turned Mira and Cael into a procession: two people followed by other versions of themselves, one older, one younger, one carrying a child, one who had never met the other. The reflections moved out of order.

In one pane Cael reached for Mira's hand before she stumbled; in another she was already falling. The future was as restless as the past.

Mira walked faster. She refused to look at the reflection in which her father appeared beside her.

He wore the blue waistcoat he had worn on the day he vanished and was looking not at the lens but at her, with the exhausted tenderness of a man who had spent his last hope badly.

At the end of the passage stood the eastern lens. It was enormous: a clear sphere held in twelve great rings, suspended over a pit that appeared to descend into the night beyond the world. Blue fires burned around its rim.

Across its surface, innumerable names were engraved in letters as small as the grains of an old photograph.

A woman was standing on the opposite side of the sphere. Her coat was the color of fog, her hair white, her body indistinct at the edges.

Mira knew her from a picture in the kitchen; in the picture she was holding a blue bowl and laughing because something beyond the frame had made her drop a spoon.

**Mira:** Mother?

The woman did not move. Her voice came from the glass, not her throat.

Voice: The lens is not a door. It is an agreement made visible. If you open it without learning the terms, the city you love will become the price of the city you save.

Mira stepped forward. Her hand passed through a patch of cold light. The figure turned into tiny stars, then reassembled at the far side of the lens, nearer than before.

**Mira:** Are you alive?

Voice: There are questions a person may answer only by choosing what they mean by the word. I am what the lens remembered of your mother. I am also a message she left in case you came here before the hour of the closing.

**Mira:** Did she leave me anything else?

Voice: She left you the belief that a daughter might become wiser than the people who were meant to keep her safe.

The words were terrible in their smallness. Mira had prepared for a warning, a confession, perhaps an indictment. She had not prepared to be spoken to as a daughter by a shape that was almost her mother.

She knew the difference between a memory and a person. It did not lessen her wish to be held.

**Cael:** We have not come to break the lens. We want to know what was promised, and to whom.

Voice: Then you must read the three records. The First contains the contract. The Second contains the names the Council removed from it. The Third contains the witness who could make the contract binding again.

You cannot bring all three records through the passage intact. It must be decided which loss you are willing to accept.

Behind the woman, three doors opened onto impossible spaces: a room of burning ledgers, a garden where every flower carried a name, and an observatory seen from beneath an ocean. Mira understood that the choices represented different kinds of evidence.

She also understood that every choice would leave something unknown.

*Choice: record witness*

## Record Witness

They descended into an observatory beneath a black sea. Fish moved between the bookshelves, as if the books had grown there instead of being placed by human hands. In the center hung a living bell. Its rim rose and fell like a sleeping chest.

When Mira touched it, the water became a room full of voices.

The Keeper of the Threshold appeared not as a monster but as a child in a silver coat, no older than seven. Its eyes held the patience of something that had waited beyond the age of stars. It had witnessed the first bargain.

It had not been permitted to record a dissent, because the language of the contract contained no provision for a witness to disagree.

Keeper: I remember those whom the Council did not ask. I cannot decide for them. But I can carry their answers across the boundary, if someone returns me to the place where I was made.

**Cael:** And where is that?

Keeper: In the heart of the instrument. Where all erased names go until the world learns to speak them again.

Mira carried the bell's small silver clapper back to the lens. She had found a witness, but not the words of the bargain or the citizens' names. The omission felt dangerous. It was also the shape of a hope.

## Fourth Chapter

The storm began at the wrong end of the sky. Lightning rose out of the clouds and vanished into the stars without striking the observatory; thunder arrived before its light. Every window flashed with a different image of the city that had been erased.

In one pane Bellwether was burning. In another it had never been built.

In a third, small children were playing a game of marbles in streets that still existed, oblivious to the fact that their existence was being argued over by people who were not present.

Mira laid her chosen evidence on the floor beneath the eastern lens. The sphere brightened. For the first time she could see beyond the glass: a market, an avenue of cypress trees, and a canal illuminated by a thousand floating candles.

AsterVale was beautiful and entirely ordinary in the ways that mattered. Its people were hurrying home before the weather, losing gloves, finding missing coins, quarreling over the price of fruit. The Council's histories had spoken of it as an abstract quantity, an instrument's burden.

It was impossible to watch the people crossing the square without recognizing how much that description had concealed.

Cael pressed his hand against the glass. His scar answered with a blue pulse, like an artery transmitting light. For an instant the surface became soft. He recoiled, then pressed again.

Mira saw a girl on the other side, perhaps twelve years old, wearing the same blue scarf she had seen in the fragment of Bellwether. She raised her hand to meet his, but the glass held a distance neither of them could measure.

**Cael:** I know her.

**Mira:** From where?

**Cael:** I know the way she frowns when she is trying to remember the name of a bird. I know the first word she ever said, and that it was not a word at all, only a sound we all pretended to understand.

**Cael:** I know she used to draw windows on the inside of boxes so that things put away would not become lonely.

He stepped back. His face had gone pale. The girl on the other side was turning away, called by a woman from somewhere behind the market.

**Cael:** She's my sister. I had a sister.

Mira had seen him smile in the early evening. Now she watched the exact moment a man discovered that the space left by a person was larger than his life.

She reached for him, then stopped when she saw the way his fingers were shaking.

**Cael:** Her name is Elian. It was Elian. I do not know whether it still is.

**Mira:** We will find out.

He looked at her with sudden fear, not for himself but for the promise she had made. It was easy to say they would find someone who had been removed from the world. Such promises could become a new way of being cruel.

An immense shadow crossed the glass. The market disappeared. The Council's seal rose in its place, followed by a figure made of gold lines: Chancellor Orsol, director of the observatory and presiding cartographer of the upper islands.

She had taught Mira mathematics when she was a child. Later, when Mira asked where her father had gone, Orsol answered with the phrase all powerful people use when they have decided a child will not remember the answer: one day you will understand.

Orsol: Mira Vale. You have opened a prohibited instrument and disturbed the agreement that keeps these islands safe. I expected better of you.

**Mira:** I expected the same of you, Chancellor. We seem to be having a disappointing evening.

Orsol: Your father's actions were necessary. He understood what you have not yet learned: a disaster averted is invisible evidence. You cannot point to the people saved from a storm that never struck and demand to see their gratitude.

**Mira:** You can ask them whether they consent to being kept outside the world. You never did.

Orsol: If the hidden cities choose to return, the storm returns with them. The outer islands will fall. Their children will drown in the clouds. This is the weight on the other side of your argument.

**Mira:** Then tell the people the truth and allow them to choose with it.

Orsol: Truth is a luxury when one has the time to debate. We have less than four hours. Close the lens. Give me the atlas. I will renegotiate the toll and delay the closing for another generation.

The offer sounded rational in exactly the way dangerous things often do when someone has carefully removed the part of the problem that cannot be solved.

Another generation meant another century of missing families, erased schools, and mothers setting places for daughters whose names were gone. The Chancellor had lived long enough to believe that postponement was a form of mercy.

**Cael:** If you know the hidden city is alive, why did you tell me that the voices I heard there were dreams?

Orsol looked at him as if seeing an inconvenient detail in an old equation.

Orsol: Because a messenger who remembers where he has been cannot be safely sent back. Your mind was not strong enough to carry the boundary. We spared you the burden.

**Cael:** You took my sister and called it mercy.

Orsol: I kept her alive.

The silence after that was more frightening than the storm. Mira understood that both claims could be true and that the truth of one did not cancel the violence of the other. The atlas was not an ethical machine.

It was a machine in a world where ethical choices had been withheld until they resembled mechanical necessities.

Orsol: I will be at the eastern stair when the last bell sounds. If you are wise, you will be there before me.

The seal collapsed into a circle of blue light. The market returned. Elian was gone.

Cael stood very still beside the glass, and Mira felt how little use it was to be right about a question when someone had just been given back a grief he had not known he possessed.

*Choice: expose route*

## Expose Route

Mira connected the eastern lens to the public signal mirrors that had once carried weather reports to every island above the clouds. She had repaired them as a girl.

The Chancellor had banned their use when the erasures began, describing public observation as an unnecessary source of alarm.

**Cael:** How many people will see this?

**Mira:** Everyone who still owns a mirror that faces east.

She transmitted the ledger of tests, the confession from her father, the name of AsterVale, and every remembered fragment of Bellwether. In distant windows the light changed. Households paused over breakfast tables that had become the wrong shape.

The voice of the city reached them as a thin music under the ordinary noise of life.

Orsol's seal appeared, attempting to block the transmission. Mira had already sent it through the old lighthouse system, whose mirrors were too simple to recognize an order from the Council.

For the first time the people of the outer world were permitted to know what had been done in their names. The knowledge did not tell them what to choose.

It made the choice their own problem, which was more than the Council had ever allowed.

## Threshold Market

The hidden city began where the eastern lens ended. Its central market occupied a wide circle beneath towers built from gold limestone, each connected to its neighbor by a bridge of dark blue metal. Lanterns hung from cables above the stalls.

Every lantern contained the name of a person who had left the city, as if the people had tried to send their absent loved ones a light without knowing whether a light could cross the boundary.

Vendors sold the ordinary necessities of life: tea, flour, shoelaces, maps of streets that ended at the city's invisible border. The people had adapted to their confinement with the inventiveness that makes it possible to live inside almost any injustice.

Children had made a game of throwing stones against the glass wall and watching them return, older than they had been when they left a hand.

The bakers traded recipes from memory because none of their old books could be read by anyone outside the boundary.

Mira stood near a fountain whose water flowed upward and read a notice on the town board. It announced a missing person's meeting, a performance by the winter choir, and the selection of delegates to the Emergency Assembly.

The last announcement was written in nervous handwriting, as though the town clerk had not expected the word emergency to become an everyday part of civic life.

Cael was searching every face. Mira did not need to ask whom he hoped to find. He had not spoken Elian's name since Orsol accused him of being unfit to remember her.

He touched the leather strap of his satchel whenever someone with a blue scarf passed.

**Mira:** There are thousands of people here. We will have to search with more than our eyes.

**Cael:** I remember where she used to sit when we bought pastries. If there is a street with a clock that runs backward, the shop may still be there.

The clock ran backward. A small boy was winding it from a ladder, laughing because he had been told the clock had always done that and saw no reason it should change.

A woman behind a counter recognized Cael's scar before she recognized his face. She put down a tray of sugared pears and began to cry with the calm certainty of someone who had waited too long to be surprised.

Baker: You're one of the messengers. We were told you could come back only if the cities agreed to forget you.

**Cael:** I didn't agree to forget anyone.

Baker: It is possible that the people who signed for you did not ask.

She told them that Elian had been taken to the House of Returns, a school for children whose families were outside the boundary.

The children were taught the names of cities they could not reach, but not the faces of the people who had loved them. The Chancellor had insisted it would be kinder not to encourage impossible hopes.

Mira looked at the row of lamps above the counter, each carrying a name in blue glass. One lamp had gone dark. Beside it, someone had set an empty saucer for a person who was expected to come home.

They walked toward the House of Returns through streets that changed names at every turn. Mira began to understand why the City of AsterVale had built such elaborate signs.

If you could not leave a place, you made the place contain enough language that it could continue to feel like a world.

At a corner near the canal they passed a procession of children carrying paper birds. A girl looked directly at Cael. She wore a blue scarf and had a small white scar above one eyebrow. He stopped in the middle of the street.

**Cael:** Elian?

The girl looked at him as though at a picture from a book whose title she could not remember. Then she reached into her pocket and brought out a window she had drawn on a folded piece of paper.

She had made it as a child to help her belongings feel less lonely. Mira recognized the detail from Cael's account. The girl was older than the one he remembered, perhaps sixteen. Time had continued for her even when memory had not.

Elian: I used to dream there was someone standing outside my room. He always had a red kite and told me it was really a bird because he couldn't make the wings stay straight.

**Cael:** It was a terrible kite.

Elian: You know it?

**Cael:** I made it.

She looked down at the folded paper. Her mouth trembled as she tried to fit a face to the shape of a memory. For a moment she seemed to be deciding whether to run away from the possibility or toward it.

Then she stepped closer and asked him a question no one else in the city could have answered.

Elian: When we were frightened of the thunder, what did we call it?

**Cael:** A giant moving the furniture upstairs.

She laughed. It was the first sound Mira had heard from her that was not an effort to remember something. Elian held out her hand, and Cael took it. The scar on his wrist glowed faintly.

The lamps above the market were still burning, but one had begun to shine brighter.

Mira turned away, not from embarrassment, but to give the reunion an ordinary space in which to happen. She was glad she had not promised him that his sister would return. She had promised only that they would find out.

The difference was finally a kindness instead of a failure.

From every bell tower came a deep metallic tone. A printed notice appeared on the market board: THE AGREEMENT WILL CLOSE AT FIRST LIGHT. ALL RESIDENTS MUST REMAIN IN THEIR DESIGNATED DISTRICTS.

The Chancellor's authority had found a way to cross the boundary without asking the people who lived behind it.

AsterVale's mayor stood before the fountain and read the notice aloud. The crowd listened in silence until a woman near the front asked whether the agreement required their consent.

The mayor admitted that no copy of the original contract had ever been shown to the city. A man selling vegetables demanded to know why a contract he had never seen could govern his grandchildren.

The murmuring grew into voices, then arguments, then the beginning of a public meeting.

Mira had spent years believing arguments were things people made when they had forgotten the value of evidence.

Watching the market, she began to suspect that an argument could also be a kind of evidence: that a person cared enough about the future to speak where a command would have preferred silence.

*Choice: storm heard*

## Storm Heard

The boundary looked like a pale wall of rain. Beyond it the outer sky seemed at once very far away and immediately adjacent, as if distance had become a matter of permission rather than measurement.

At the edge of the canal, Mira found an old telescope pointed not toward the stars but toward a region of darkness below the horizon.

She looked through it and saw the storm. It was not a cloud, and not a weather system. It was a pressure between histories: the friction of two worlds kept from occupying the same space.

Wherever the lens moved, one version of a street appeared over another. A house could stand and fall in the same instant; a child could be born and never conceived, and both possibilities pulled against the glass with the force of an ocean.

**Cael:** If we open the passage, those worlds will meet.

**Mira:** Yes. But the storm isn't waiting outside the cities. The storm is what happens when we insist on keeping them apart.

The realization changed the question she would ask at the assembly. It was not whether the outer islands could survive a natural disaster. It was whether the instrument had become the disaster it was supposed to prevent.

## Assembly

The Emergency Assembly met beneath the open sky, because the Council chamber had become a place where people expected lies to be spoken in comfortable chairs. It was a disorderly meeting.

There were questions from bakers, teachers, pilots, and children who did not understand why grown people had allowed a city to remain hidden without first deciding what would happen when it wanted to return. The mayor began with a prepared speech.

A woman carrying a bucket interrupted to ask why the water was running backward. The mayor put away the speech.

Orsol appeared in the eastern lens, enormous as a mountain and as carefully composed as a portrait in a government building. She offered another generation of safety in exchange for the atlas and its evidence. The citizens could return to their work.

The outer islands could continue to imagine themselves innocent of the agreement. The toll would be paid by places selected according to mathematical criteria. Nobody would need to know which places until after they were gone.

A child asked whether anyone would remember his birthday. Orsol said the question was sentimental. A hundred people began to speak at once.

Mira had once believed that the opposite of secrecy was disclosure. In the market she learned that disclosure was only the beginning. Facts placed in the hands of people who could not act on them were another form of decoration.

The people needed time, language, and the knowledge that their votes could change something. They had been denied all three.

Mayor: We have seventeen thousand residents. The time remaining would not allow a ballot taken from every household. We can send three delegates, elected here and witnessed by the outer world, to speak for the city. But their decision must be limited.

No one has the right to make an irreversible bargain that the living cannot revise.

**Mira:** That is exactly the principle the Council refused to put in the contract.

Orsol: A city cannot vote to undo the laws of physics.

**Mira:** It can vote to stop calling your decision a law of physics.

The Chancellor's face tightened. Mira realized how rarely anyone had addressed her without first asking permission to speak.

Orsol looked older than she had when Mira was a child, and yet the same in the precise way a clock looks the same after years of measuring other people's lives.

Elian stepped onto the fountain. She was young, and the crowd fell quiet not because youth gave her authority but because the people understood she had lived the consequence of a decision made before she could consent.

Elian: When I was little, I was told that my brother had chosen to leave. Later I was told I had imagined him. For years I was ashamed of remembering somebody who supposedly didn't exist.

I have lived here all my life and I love this city. I don't want to lose it. But if keeping it means teaching another girl that her brother never loved her, I don't want that bargain to continue in my name.

Cael stood below the fountain with both hands closed at his sides. Mira saw him trying not to make the moment about his grief. She admired him for it more than she could have explained. Elian had spoken for herself.

He had not interrupted to provide a better version of her story.

A teacher spoke about the schools and the libraries that would be lost if the cities collapsed back into the outside world. A gardener spoke about the children who had been born inside the boundary, who did not remember any other sky.

A pilot argued that the storm might be navigable if it could be studied rather than feared. A mother asked why the choice had to be between an everlasting prison and a catastrophe that killed everyone.

That was the question the machine did not know how to answer. Its terms were binary: keep the world divided or pay the debt by reunifying it. The instrument had no provision for a third way.

The Council had built a government around that limitation, and for seventeen years no one had been permitted to question whether the limitation belonged to the universe or merely to the instrument.

Mira remembered her father's letter. He had written that he had failed to make the erasure gentle. He had not written that he failed to make it reversible. The distinction felt intentional.

A person who knew how to build a device sometimes left the most important part of the instructions in the form of an apology.

She asked the delegates to wait while she returned to the eastern lens. The mayor objected that they had little time; Orsol objected that time had already run out. Mira answered that neither of them would know until someone asked the machine.

Cael volunteered to accompany her.

The delegates were chosen. There was a representative of the families, a representative of the occupations that kept the city alive, and a child chosen by the other children to speak about the future they expected to inhabit.

Mira was relieved they had not chosen her. She had come to give the hidden city a voice, not to acquire one more place where hers counted most.

The sky above the market bent into a pale ring. The atlas began pulling at its brass hinges, as if a great tide had formed between its pages. The closing had begun earlier than predicted.

Orsol's projection faded, and in the silence that followed Mira could hear the frightened breathing of the people around her.

**Cael:** We have to go now.

**Mira:** Then we go with what they've told us. No other story will be accepted as their answer.

They returned through the lens carrying the elected delegation's instructions and the knowledge that the people of AsterVale had already begun deciding what to do with a future that had been withheld from them. Mira kept thinking of the child's question about birthdays.

The sky was full of grand instruments. None of them could tell her whether anyone would remember the next birthday.

## Return To Lens

The observatory was no longer stable. Its floors had developed a slow inclination toward the eastern lens, as if the building had begun sliding down a slope no architect had calculated. Doors opened on rooms they did not lead to.

A staircase from the music hall appeared in the star library; the maintenance corridor ended at a window through which Mira could see her childhood kitchen. Her mother was putting salt on the sill. The scene lasted one heartbeat.

Then the window looked out on lightning again.

Cael carried the atlas under one arm and the delegation's sealed account under the other. The paper had grown heavy.

Every word it contained represented something the machine's old agreement did not know how to hold: a right to decide, a right to revise, a right to change one's mind even after a safe choice had become a dangerous tradition.

Mira took the charred contract if she had it, or the names, or the witness's clapper, and placed it beside the atlas. The three records had not become more complete during the journey. But the missing parts now had witnesses in the world.

That mattered more than the machine expected.

She examined the base of the eastern lens. Behind a panel bearing her father's name she found a second mechanism: an unfinished ring of twelve slots, one empty, eleven filled with little glass bottles. In each bottle floated a word.

She could read some of them. Mercy. Silence. Debt. Arrival. Home. The final bottle, smallest and nearest the empty slot, contained the word Again.

**Mira:** It's a grammar.

**Cael:** I beg your pardon?

**Mira:** The instrument isn't just a device. It constructs a statement about the world. We are living in the conclusion of a sentence written seventeen years ago. The missing slot is where the sentence can be changed.

She opened her father's maintenance notes. Between diagrams of gears were fragments of an abandoned language in which a command became binding only when spoken by two parties who understood the same word differently.

Her father had tried to design an opening clause that would allow the agreement to be renewed by those who were excluded. The Chancellor had forbidden him to complete it.

**Cael:** What would filling the slot do?

**Mira:** Give the city the chance to write a new clause. But the machine has to recognize the voices. It recognizes my father's blood, your scar, and the delegates' authority. It might accept us as witnesses if we can bring them to the lens.

**Cael:** The delegates are at the market. We can fetch them.

**Mira:** And Orsol will be at the eastern stair when the bell strikes. If she reaches the control ring first, she can lock the clause again. We may not have time to do both.

Cael looked toward the door. The stonework of the observatory shook. A coil of copper fell from the ceiling.

He caught it before it struck the atlas, then stared at the metal in his hands as if it had reminded him of something he had been unable to say.

**Cael:** I know this ring. I was here when it was built. I wasn't a child. I was the messenger your father called to carry the first words through the lens. We thought we were saving the cities.

**Cael:** When the toll began taking names, I tried to tell them. They made me forget so that I would keep returning with another message.

**Mira:** You remember now?

**Cael:** Pieces. I remember carrying a letter addressed to you when you were seven. I remember Elian crying because I could not stay. I remember your father burning the compass into my wrist. He apologized while he did it.

**Cael:** I told him the apology would not matter unless he stopped.

The room grew quiet around them. Mira had often imagined her father as a victim of the Council. Now she saw that his grief had come with responsibilities. He had tried to repair the harm. He had also caused it.

Love and culpability were not opposites; a person could carry both, and the people who loved him could be asked to carry the knowledge without being asked to excuse it.

She looked into the lens. Her mother's remembered figure stood far away in the darkness, not moving. Mira wanted to ask whether her father had ever found a way to forgive himself. She did not.

The question would have been another attempt to make the dead answer for the living.

*Choice: write clause*

## Write Clause

Mira placed her palm on the empty bottle and spoke the word that her father had left unspoken: Again. The ring answered with a vibration so deep it seemed to arise from the bones of the observatory.

The eleven filled bottles began to exchange their words, forming combinations Mira could almost read. The lens displayed the cities as overlapping diagrams, their streets aligned at the places where people on either side still remembered one another.

Cael touched the compass scar. His voice entered the machine not as a command but as a witness. He described the red kite, the backward clock, the bakery where he had first learned to make a poor joke at the right time.

The lens recorded his memories without erasing them. Mira realized the instrument could hold two histories at once when neither insisted on making the other disappear.

**Mira:** We were taught that the machine can keep or release the cities. What if its real function is to remember the boundary without forcing us to live inside it?

The silver ring flickered. For an instant she saw a third version of the sky, one in which the hidden cities remained visible and connected to the outer world by bridges of light. Then the vision collapsed.

The machine required the voices of the city to make it stable. The delegates would still have to choose whether to complete the clause.

## Final Engine

The last bell began to ring. It did not stop. Its sound moved through the observatory as a physical force, breaking the remaining windows and lifting pages into the air.

The atlas rose from the table and opened to the blank place where Bellwether had been. Each vanished name appeared for one instant in a halo of blue light, then fell backward into the pages like rain.

The eastern lens expanded until it filled the room. AsterVale became visible behind it, enormous and impossibly near. Mira could see the market square, the House of Returns, the backward clock, and Elian's blue scarf moving through a crowd.

The city was no longer something she could place on a map. It was a place full of people asking to be allowed to remain real.

Orsol stood beside the main control ring. She had the official seal in one hand, but its light had faded. The delegates, or their written instructions, had reached the observatory.

Their words appeared around the lens, not as a single decision but as several demands: the right to remain, the right to leave, the right to remember, the right to refuse a rescue that was indistinguishable from imprisonment.

**Cael:** It's listening.

**Mira:** No. It is waiting for someone to give it a command. We need it to understand that listening is different.

On the far side of the glass stood the Keeper of the Threshold. It wore the same small silver coat, and its face was older than any face should be allowed to become while still resembling a child.

It pointed to the empty slot in the twelvefold ring.

Keeper: The first agreement was a statement about what must be lost. The new one must be a statement about what can be shared. But every statement has a cost. Someone will have to teach the machine a word it has never understood.

Mira looked down at her father's watch in Cael's hand, at the ledger of names, at the page of the contract or the clapper of the witness's bell. None was a perfect tool.

All had become useful because someone had refused to allow them to be the last word.

The lens displayed four outcomes. In the first, the cities rejoined the world and the storm was allowed to pass through them, though not without danger. In the second, a living witness entered the instrument and became the memory that held the passage open.

In the third, the Council's agreement was renewed for one final generation under public supervision, preserving the world as it was while the citizens prepared for a safer return.

In the fourth, the instrument was destroyed; no bargain could be made again, and the world would have to bear both the missing places and the responsibility for finding them by ordinary means.

**Cael:** Which of those is the right choice?

**Mira:** I don't know. I used to think the purpose of studying the stars was to learn enough that I would never have to say that. Now I think the stars have been trying to teach me how to say it properly.

Orsol raised her face toward the lens. The Chancellor had spent her life believing that a person in authority was obliged to be certain. She looked at the four possibilities and, for the first time, did not announce that one of them was necessary.

Orsol: The city has given us its conditions. What do you propose to write into the final clause?

Mira looked at Cael. He looked back without attempting to choose for her. The atlas was open, its blank places waiting. Beyond the windows the first light of an unfinished morning touched the clouds.

Mira understood then that an ending was not a place where a story ceased. It was a place where the living accepted that the consequences of their choices would outlast the sentences in which they described them.

*Choice: ending witness*

## Ending Witness

Mira chose the second outcome because she could see no other way to protect both histories without asking anyone else to disappear. The machine required a witness who would remain inside it, continuously naming the connections between the hidden cities and the world outside.

A person could become that witness. Their body would be held in the glass. Their voice would travel along the bridges, keeping the passage open as long as they could remember.

**Cael:** No. There has to be another way.

**Mira:** There may be. I don't have the right to make everyone wait for me to find it.

He turned away, then came back with an anger that frightened her because it was so carefully controlled. He had lost his sister once to a rescue he had not agreed to.

He could not watch another person volunteer to vanish into the mechanism that had taken her.

**Cael:** You are describing yourself as though you were only a useful piece of equipment.

**Mira:** I am describing a choice I have made.

**Cael:** Then let me tell you what that choice costs the people who love you. It costs them your ordinary voice and your terrible habit of leaving ink on every surface.

**Cael:** It costs them your questions after midnight and the way you look at the sky as though it owes you an argument. Don't call that a small price because the machine knows how to measure it.

Mira listened. She did not change her mind. She did change the words she intended to speak. If she were to enter the lens, she would do so without pretending that becoming useful made her less human.

She asked the elected delegates whether they would accept a passage kept open by a living witness. The delegates conferred.

The child representing the future demanded that the witness be permitted to speak from inside the lens and that the agreement contain a provision for release if another method could be found. The adults agreed.

Mira wrote the clause in language the instrument could understand and the people could revise.

The glass opened around her. The first sensation was of being remembered by something enormous. Every name Mira had ever read passed through her, then every name she had forgotten, then the names of people she could not have known.

There were children on islands beyond the maps and people asleep in cities that had been hidden for centuries. Their histories did not merge. They became visible beside one another, like separate books placed on a shelf.

Mira stood in a room made of starlight. The eastern lens was a window in each wall. She could see AsterVale, Bellwether, the observatory, and the seas below the clouds. She could not leave the room.

The new clause had placed her there by choice, but choice did not make the walls less real.

She heard Cael calling her name. At first his voice came from a distance that made the room seem impossibly large.

Then she found the place in the glass where her palm met his, and the distance shortened until she could see every line on his face.

**Mira:** I am here.

**Cael:** I know. I'm going to find a way to make those words mean you can come out.

**Mira:** Then I will keep a record of every attempt, including the failed ones. Especially the failed ones.

For a while the arrangement worked. The hidden cities reopened their passages. Their residents traveled between the islands with passports issued by both governments. The storm remained beyond the boundary, but its pressure became a weather pattern rather than a catastrophe.

Mira's presence in the instrument prevented the histories from colliding and gave the people time to build bridges that were not made only of light.

The cost was loneliness. She could visit every part of the world through the lens but could touch almost none of it. Each morning she watched the market open in AsterVale and wondered what fresh bread smelled like.

Each evening she watched the observatory library darken and remembered how the polished marble felt cold beneath her feet. She had chosen the sacrifice. She was not obliged to enjoy every part of it.

Cael visited as often as the sky routes allowed. Sometimes he brought news about Elian, who had become a teacher and was organizing the records of the House of Returns into a public archive.

Sometimes he brought gossip about the Chancellor's trial or the latest argument between the islands about the taxation of bridges. Sometimes he brought nothing at all and sat beside the glass while Mira spoke about the star patterns only she could see.

One winter evening he told her he loved her. He did not put the words in the shape of a plea. Mira had spent her life becoming suspicious of promises that asked another person to change their choices.

She answered that she loved him too, which was both true and infuriatingly impractical. They laughed at the cruelty of timing, and then they began planning a future neither would promise the other was guaranteed.

Years passed. The observation room acquired a chair made from a tree that had grown in Bellwether before the erasure.

It was a poor chair, because wood brought across a memory boundary did not hold its shape perfectly, but Mira loved the feel of it. A young engineer from AsterVale discovered how to send objects into the lens without paying a toll.

The first successful object was a cup of tea. Mira cried when she tasted it, then was embarrassed and cried again.

The engineer returned with a team. They found that the lens had been designed to require a witness only because the Council's old agreement made memory a private resource.

Once the people of the connected cities began sharing their histories in public archives, the machine could distribute the work of witnessing. Mira's task became smaller. One day she found she could step outside the glass for the length of a breath.

She did not leave that day. The possibility frightened her more than she had expected. She had lived in the room long enough to make its limits part of her idea of herself.

Freedom, she discovered, could require a kind of courage that sacrifice did not. In the following weeks she took one step, then two, then the length of the eastern gallery. Cael stood beside the door and refused to count aloud.

The Keeper of the Threshold helped design the final opening. It insisted that memory ought never to depend on one person alone. The new system drew strength from the names publicly read by the schools, libraries, and families of both worlds.

The Keeper called this a chorus rather than an agreement, and Mira thought the word was better.

On the morning she finally walked into the observatory without a wall of glass between her and the world, the building was much changed. Vines had grown through the western windows. The upper gallery had been repaired with wood from a hidden city's trees.

The table where the atlas had first opened of its own accord stood near the door, scarred and covered in the kind of disorder that means people are working.

Mira put her hand on the marble. It was cold. She laughed because the ordinary sensation made her more grateful than any of the stars she had learned to see.

## Epilogue Witness

The cities created a new office called the Department of Shared Memory. Its primary purpose was to prevent anyone from making an agreement on behalf of people who had not been asked.

Its secondary purpose was to archive contradictory accounts without forcing them to agree. Mira served as its first director for two years, after which she resigned because she had been offered the chance to travel on a skyship that was not entirely reliable.

At the farewell dinner Cael returned her a watch he had repaired. It still had no hands. He said it was a reminder that time did not belong to the instruments that claimed to measure it. Mira wore it anyway.

The final page of her account contained one sentence: I was not wrong to give myself for the world; I would have been wrong to believe the world required me to remain lost.

The End: THE LIVING WITNESS.

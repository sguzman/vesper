# The Atlas of Unwritten Skies

*Complete story path: Solstice Covenant*

## Prologue

The last map in the world had a hole in it. Not a tear: torn paper left fibers, a visible raggedness where the knife had passed. The Atlas of Unwritten Skies was intact.

Its vellum shone like a shallow pond at sunrise, its coastlines remained fine as hairs, its copper letters had not dimmed in three hundred years.

But where the city of Bellwether should have stood, someone had rubbed away the fact that a city had ever existed.

The observatory had always insisted that the sky was legible. Every instrument had a purpose, every shelf a number, and every mistake a procedure for correction. That morning, before Cael arrived, Mira had discovered a different kind of mistake in the oldest catalog.

The book contained an index for eight hundred and twelve entries, but between the headings for tides and translation there were seven empty pages, each numbered in a careful copper hand. The missing entries occupied space. They had not left their subjects behind.

She had held the book up to the window and seen impressions in the paper where someone had pressed a pen hard enough to mark the leaves beneath.

On page three, a sentence was still partly visible: A HOUSE IS A KIND OF PROMISE MADE BY A STREET. On page four, in a different script: DO NOT LET THE MINISTERS CALL IT WEATHER.

The next impression was the shape of a child's hand, traced around five fingers. She copied the marks into her own ledger, knowing that writing them might preserve their shapes but not what they meant.

Her first impulse had been to report the discovery to the Council. The Council kept records, employed archivists, maintained centuries of observations, and paid Mira's salary when it remembered to pay anyone.

Yet she could name three researchers who had sent warnings to the Council and become the subjects of warnings themselves. They were not in prison. That would at least have left a place where letters might be sent.

Their rooms had been reassigned, their names removed from the rosters, and their colleagues trained to say that the vacancies were administrative matters. The Council had developed a remarkably efficient vocabulary for turning human beings into scheduling problems.

Mira set the ledger beside the atlas. She imagined the city as its inhabitants must have last seen it: morning bread cooling on a sill, a window opened for the smell of rain, an argument paused because someone had to leave for work.

A disappearance was easy to think about when it belonged to a map. She was learning to think about the breakfast that went uneaten and the person who would never return to finish the argument. That was why she had stopped sleeping.

It was also why she could not stop counting.

Mira Vale had known Bellwether in the ordinary way people know places they hope to return to.

She remembered wet pavement and a window full of pears, an argument about whether the sea was green or blue, and her father's hand holding hers so she would not cross when the tram bell sounded.

Now there was a patch of cream-colored paper in her father's atlas. She could remember him. She could no longer remember the sound of his voice.

The omission grew by one narrow street whenever the observatory clock struck twelve. Mira had taken to avoiding clocks. It had not helped.

**Mira:** There was a bakery here. A blue door and a woman who insisted that the best bread was always sold out. Her husband played the violin badly, and everyone loved him for it.

She touched the blank place with the bone tip of a measuring instrument. The ink beside her fingertip shivered. A dot of coastline slipped away, leaving the harbor suspended without water.

She had begun counting the losses in a notebook, because the losses did not count themselves.

The observatory stood on an arch of stone above a country of clouds. Its libraries occupied twelve stories; its lenses, suspended in revolving brass, were larger than rooms.

Every evening the clouds below reflected the colors of a sunrise that would not occur for another ten hours. Some called this an optical marvel. Mira called it an intolerable habit of the sky, which ought to keep a more straightforward schedule.

In her ledger she had written: Seven bridges. Two hospitals. An orchard. The names of four hundred and seventeen people.

For the last three nights she had written the same sentence underneath the list: I must find out who is doing this before the list learns to include me.

A small ember of blue light escaped the great celestial sphere above her. It drifted between brass rings, hesitated over the ledger, and settled on the drawing of the vanished harbor. Mira reached for it.

Instead of heat, she felt the cold pressure of an unfamiliar memory: a boy laughing in a stairwell, a lantern breaking in a fountain, and a name spoken too softly to hear.

The pressure faded. The blue spark remained, trapped beneath her fingertip like the reflection of a tiny star.

**Mira:** Who are you supposed to be?

The west door rang, which was alarming. No one came to the west door.

For thirty years it had opened onto a staircase that was not attached to a building, and people who attempted the stairs usually found themselves on a rooftop they had never visited.

The door rang again with the persistence of somebody who had been told to wait and had decided waiting was merely an inefficient way of knocking.

Mira closed the atlas. She did not extinguish the lamp. There are ways to make a room seem unoccupied and ways to make it seem dangerous. She preferred the second.

## Courier

The man outside wore the weather of three separate altitudes. His navy cloak had been soaked by rain and dried by wind; its hem had acquired a white rim of frost. A leather satchel rested against his hip.

His boots were old but carefully repaired, and a telescope hung from a strap beside a pocket watch without hands. He looked at Mira's expression, then at the iron hook in her hand.

**Cael:** I have a delivery, but I can come back when the room is less committed to self-defense.

**Mira:** The post office stopped delivering to the observatory eleven years ago.

**Cael:** The post office stopped existing eleven years ago. Not quite the same thing.

The man offered an envelope without stepping over the threshold. Across it, in a script Mira knew better than her own, was a sentence that made the entire building seem to tilt: TO THE DAUGHTER I WILL NO LONGER REMEMBER.

She stared at it so long that the courier slowly lowered his arm. He did not joke again.

**Mira:** Where did you find this?

**Cael:** In Bellwether. Under the south platform, where the tracks ended in the sea. I have been carrying it for eight days. Each morning I woke with fewer memories of the place I had collected it.

**Cael:** By yesterday I was not sure which direction the platform faced. Today I cannot remember the station at all, only the letter. I thought I had gone mad until I saw the hole in the map from your balcony.

**Mira:** You can see it from there?

**Cael:** There are lights under the cloudline. Not stars; stars would have the good sense to remain where they were put. Your observatory has a blue wound in its roof and all the birds avoid it.

Mira had never asked a stranger to describe the observatory from outside. She had never considered that its emptiness might be visible. The idea made her feel suddenly as if she had been living with a wound without noticing the blood on her clothes.

He said his name was Cael Vey. He had once worked the sky routes that threaded islands into the upper air. His company had gone bankrupt when the northern winds changed. A week later the company had not existed.

His colleagues vanished from the records, although their signatures remained on his old pay slips. He kept the slips folded in oilskin, not for their monetary value, but to have the signatures of people who had once addressed him by name.

**Cael:** I used to think maps were promises that the world would keep its appointments. Now I think the world has been refusing to attend for some time.

**Mira:** The world is not refusing. Someone is cancelling the appointments.

Cael considered her for a moment, as though deciding whether she was dangerous because she knew something or because she knew too little. Then he held out the envelope again.

**Cael:** In that case, I would prefer not to be late.

Mira recognized the seal. An eight-pointed compass star impressed in dark blue wax: her father's private mark, used when he wanted to tell her something the Council would never allow him to publish.

She had last seen it seventeen years ago, when he disappeared into the storm beyond the western observatory.

The paper trembled in her hands. A strange delicacy was required to handle a letter from the dead. She wanted to tear it open with her teeth.

She also wanted to put it back in its envelope and live forever in the moment before knowing what it contained.

## Protective Choice

Mira examined the wax, held it over a lamp to check for false impressions, and tested the paper against the observatory's oldest catalog. The ink proved genuine.

The seal contained a fleck of blue glass from the workshop where her father had once repaired instruments, a detail no one could have forged without knowing the house where she grew up.

Cael watched without objecting. The patience was worse than suspicion; if he had been angry, Mira could have accused him of manipulation. Instead he stood with both hands visible and let her establish the truth at her own speed.

**Mira:** When I find out you have lied, I will have you locked in a storeroom full of failed telescopes.

**Cael:** Those are the only interesting kind.

She opened the letter. It named her father as an accomplice in the erasures and warned against the eastern lens. Mira laid it on the table and felt the weight of a fact that no lock could protect her from.

## The Letters Never Sent

The envelope lay between them, its blue wax broken and the edges of the paper worn thin by the fingers of people who had tried not to read it. Mira had expected a single confession.

Now the letter seemed less like an answer than a key to a room whose existence had been deliberately concealed. She turned it toward the lamp and saw a second set of marks impressed beneath the ink. They were not visible letters.

They were the pressure of letters that had been written on a page above it and then torn away.

Cael found a soft piece of charcoal in the drawer where Mira kept her instruments. She asked where he had learned the technique.

He said he had once traveled with a woman who repaired old documents for a living, and that she had shown him how to make hidden impressions visible without cutting the paper. He could not remember her name.

He remembered that she hated when people called the work magic, because magic was what they named a skill when they did not wish to learn how it was done.

They laid the envelope over a clean sheet and rubbed charcoal lightly across its surface. Words appeared in a narrow column: AsterVale, eastern passage, authorized messenger, last opportunity. Beneath them was a date and a line in the handwriting of Mira's mother.

The sentence was incomplete. It began: IF THE COUNCIL FORGETS THE CHILDREN, THE CHILDREN MUST...

Mira stared at the unfinished line. She had thought of her mother as a person who made tea, folded paper stars, and corrected the astronomers when their instruments contradicted the sky. She had never imagined her mother writing in the margins of Council correspondence.

For seventeen years the absence of her parents had belonged to one story: her father vanished into the western storm; her mother died before Mira was old enough to understand the meaning of death.

Now the paper suggested they had both been involved in a struggle the official history had never admitted existed.

**Mira:** She knew something. My mother knew about the agreement.

**Cael:** Perhaps she knew enough to be afraid of it.

**Mira:** She didn't tell me.

**Cael:** You were seven.

**Mira:** That is not an answer. It is the beginning of every answer adults gave when they wanted me to stop asking.

She was angry at her mother for dying before the question could be asked, angry at her father for leaving behind an apology instead of an explanation, and angry at herself for wanting the letter to restore a childhood that had never been as

simple as she remembered. The anger made her hands shake. She set down the charcoal before she damaged the page.

Cael moved to the window. Below the observatory, clouds passed through a valley where the Council's old maps showed open sea. He told her about the first letter he had ever carried as a courier.

It had been written by a woman who wanted her brother to forgive her for leaving their family's farm. The address was nearly illegible. The ship's captain had advised him to throw the letter away.

Cael had traveled an extra day to find the place, only to learn that the brother had moved to another island. He had followed the new address and arrived two weeks later with the letter crumpled and smelling of salt.

The brother read it in silence, then asked whether Cael could carry a reply. The reply was only two sentences. Cael remembered the first clearly: THE DOOR IS STILL OPEN. He could not remember the second.

He had been troubled by that for years, because he feared the forgotten words were the ones the sister most needed to hear.

**Mira:** You did what you could. The words weren't yours to keep forever.

**Cael:** I know. That is easier to say when there is a person at the other end who can remember them.

He took a small packet from his satchel. It contained three letters that had lost their destinations. One was addressed to a grandmother whose name had faded.

Another began with a child's drawing of a fish and ended with a request for a birthday cake. The third was written on blue paper, and the only legible words were: I HAVE NOT STOPPED LOOKING.

He said he carried them because someone had to decide what to do with an undeliverable message, and he had never found a rule that satisfied him.

Mira unfolded the child's letter. The drawing was uneven but enthusiastic, a fish with too many fins and an eye almost as large as its body.

She remembered making similar drawings in her mother's kitchen while waiting for the weather to clear enough to visit Bellwether. The memory came with a smell of burned sugar. She could not recall whether the cake had been good.

She could recall being loved by the person who had made it.

They opened the observatory's box for undelivered correspondence. It was an old wooden chest with a brass plate reading RETURN TO SENDER, though the clerks had rarely been able to find a sender for the items deposited there.

Inside lay receipts, love letters, notices of death, invoices for repairs, and the first pages of books abandoned before they were finished. Each item had a practical reason for being kept.

Together they felt like a record of the small bridges people built between rooms they could not occupy at the same time.

Mira found a letter addressed to the observatory itself. Its writer had been a carpenter who wanted permission to repair the western staircase after it began opening onto places that did not exist.

The Council had refused because the staircase was considered a historical feature. The carpenter's final sentence read: A BUILDING IS NOT MORE IMPORTANT THAN THE PEOPLE WHO FALL DOWN ITS STAIRS. Mira smiled despite herself.

She made a note to find the carpenter's family if the staircase ever became a normal staircase again.

Cael asked whether she wanted to keep the packet of letters. She said they should stay together until they knew more. She added her father's envelope, then stopped. The letter had been addressed to her.

It belonged to her even if the Council had once considered its contents a matter of state. She took it back out. The distinction was small, but it mattered. Recovering the truth did not require every private grief to become a public document.

The blue spark on the table moved toward the letters and paused over the child's drawing. For a moment the fish seemed to swim beneath the paper. A second spark emerged from the atlas, then a third.

They arranged themselves above the envelope in the shape of a constellation that had not appeared in any observatory chart. Mira reached for her instrument, then put it down. She wanted to see what happened before she tried to measure it.

The sparks rose toward the great globe. The brass rings turned without being touched. A soft light gathered over the place where Bellwether should have been. Cael stepped back, the packet of letters held against his chest as though they might escape.

Mira watched the atlas open and realized that the letters were not the only messages being carried through the world. The machine had been speaking for years. They had only just begun to notice the language it used.

## The Library Of Lost Afternoons

Mira did not sleep after reading her father's confession. The letter was folded and placed beneath a brass paperweight on her desk, and every time she crossed the room she caught herself checking that it was still there.

The contents had not changed, but she had begun to fear that the moment she looked away the instrument would discover a way to make her believe they had.

The observatory's smallest library occupied a circular landing above the public reading room.

It was known as the library of lost afternoons because the windows faced west and the clock had been broken for so long that nobody could recall which afternoon had been the last one it measured accurately.

As a child Mira had spent hours there inventing distant cities out of the names printed on old survey charts. Her father had brought her bread wrapped in the pages of abandoned calculations.

She had thought this proof that he considered her more important than mathematics.

Now the shelves seemed to hold a different kind of evidence. The geography books stood in a row, each with a narrow pale rectangle on its spine where a label had once been.

Her own childhood atlas lay on its side at the lowest shelf, a green cloth volume with its corners turned down.

She remembered a conversation in which her mother had told her not to straighten the corners because her father used them to mark the pages he could not bear to revise.

**Cael:** You are making the face of someone about to interrogate a book.

**Mira:** Books are usually more forthcoming than people.

**Cael:** That has not been my experience. I have delivered letters that were quite determined not to answer their own questions.

She showed him the green atlas. Its earliest pages contained drawings of real places: the south docks, the garden walk, a small staircase behind the kitchen, and a roof over which gulls had flown before the cloud shelf grew too thick to see them.

Later pages contained impossible places, or places she now knew had been removed. There was a market where the awnings had been made from the wings of discarded maps. There was a school with an enormous glass chimney.

There was a street on which every lamp cast two shadows and the second shadow arrived several minutes after the person who had made it.

She had drawn these as a child, before she was old enough to understand the machine's work. That troubled her more than the details themselves. If she had seen the places and had been made to forget them, the childish drawings were eyewitness testimony.

If she had invented them, they were another kind of evidence: proof that a mind could imagine what authority had insisted was impossible. She could not decide which conclusion she wanted to be true.

The stairs creaked beneath footsteps. An elderly librarian emerged carrying a wooden tray with three cups of tea.

Her name was Renne, though Mira had never known whether it was her given name or one chosen after her first name vanished from a register during the catastrophe. Renne had assisted her father for years.

She had refused a pension on the grounds that the library might one day require someone who remembered where the damaged books were hidden.

Renne: One of you looks as if you've read something terrible. The other looks as if he is wondering whether he has been invited to stay.

**Cael:** I was invited to help carry books. I do not know whether that extends to tea.

Renne: Everyone who carries a book properly gets tea. Everyone who drops one gets tea as well, though in a less comfortable chair.

The third cup remained untouched. Renne set it beside the little clock, where its steam floated upward through a place that did not seem to disturb the air. Mira asked for whom it had been poured. Renne's hand paused.

She said she had always made three cups in this room and that no one had ever complained about a surplus. She looked at the empty chair beneath the clock.

Its cushion was worn in a place where a small person might have sat with their feet too short to reach the floor.

Mira remembered her mother wearing a blue shawl and reading a children's book aloud. She remembered a laugh, too low and rough to have belonged to the voice in the story.

But the chair in her memory stood on the other side of the room. The chair in front of her seemed to have been moved there deliberately, as if its old place had become too dangerous for it to occupy.

Renne: Your mother kept a second journal here. Your father thought the first had burned. I let him continue thinking so. Sometimes protecting a book from its author is the only way to make sure it reaches its reader.

**Mira:** Why didn't you give it to me?

Renne: Because I was afraid you would ask whether I had chosen rightly, and I knew I hadn't earned the right to answer for you. This was not a generous fear.

From the underside of the third shelf Renne removed a narrow drawer. Its false back was painted the same uneven brown as the wood around it. Inside lay a journal bound in pale blue cloth, stitched badly along the spine.

Several pages bore water stains. One contained a pressed white flower whose stem had crumbled into a long green line. Mira knew the flower.

It had grown beside the stairs leading to the old eastern wing, where her mother had taken her to watch the evening sky change color.

The journal did not record the catastrophe as a single event. It recorded a series of days in which small things stopped agreeing with one another. A delivery of fruit arrived from a town no one could find on the official chart.

A passenger train appeared at the terminus carrying six people whose tickets had been issued on a date that the timetable insisted did not exist.

At the observatory a young apprentice began waking each morning with a memory of another life, including a child he had never met. The apprentice wrote the child's name on his wrist to preserve it.

Later the name was covered by the burn of an eight-pointed seal.

Cael leaned close enough to read the line. Mira watched his hand move toward the scar and stop. He had been speaking easily until then.

Now his expression changed not to recognition but to the dread of a person who suspects the world has arranged a recognition for him in advance.

**Cael:** What was the apprentice's name?

**Mira:** My mother doesn't say. There's a sketch. A young man with dark hair, standing beside a little girl in a coat with a blue collar. The drawing isn't good enough for me to tell whether it was you.

**Cael:** She may not have been drawing a face. She may have been trying to draw the feeling of someone being present before there was a record of him.

Renne lit another lamp. The flame reflected in the journal's water stain, briefly turning it into a map of tiny golden islands. Mira turned to the final entry.

Her mother had written that she was no longer willing to participate in the eastern lens trials.

She believed the machine could save cities from the storm, but only by treating the people who lived there as entries that could be revised without asking them.

She had begged the researchers to show their work to the families whose names appeared in the calculations. They had called her concerns sentimental and told her that a perfect instrument required an imperfect sacrifice.

Below the entry was a short list in her mother's hand. It began with the name of a baker and ended with the name of a child. Between them were the names of several assistants, a ferry keeper, and a woman called Elian Vey.

Cael read the last name twice. He whispered the words as if they belonged to a language he had known before he knew how to speak.

**Cael:** Vey. That is my name.

**Mira:** It is. But it may not be the whole of it.

**Cael:** I can remember a child holding the sleeve of my coat. I can remember promising that I'd come back for her. Every time I try to see her face, I remember a different promise instead.

**Cael:** This one was made to a woman who told me that if I delivered one more letter, the storm would spare the city. I delivered it. I don't remember what happened to the city.

Mira closed the journal. She could have asked him to tell her the promise again, to compare every word against her father's records. Instead she asked whether he wanted to sit down. Cael nodded once.

His hands shook as he took the tea Renne had poured. He did not spill any of it. Mira thought that this was probably the most difficult thing he had done since entering the room.

The old clock rang. It had not possessed a bell for thirty years. The sound was small and wooden, like someone knocking from inside a closed cupboard. All three looked up.

The hands, which had always pointed at twenty minutes after four, moved forward by the breadth of a hair. Behind the glass, a single line appeared on the face of the clock: AFTERNOONS CANNOT BE STORED. THEY MUST BE LIVED.

Renne laughed, not because the message was funny but because she had been responsible for dusting the clock for decades. She said she had tried many times to make it behave.

It had ignored her until this evening, when two young people had sat with a sorrow that neither could solve by examining a diagram. Mira found herself laughing too. The laughter startled Cael, and after a moment he joined them.

They sat there until the tea grew cold.

Renne told them how Mira's mother had taught the librarians to record a book's absence as carefully as its presence: who had borrowed it, what they hoped to learn, and whether they brought it back with a different understanding.

That practice had saved more records than the Council's official index because an erasure could remove a title but could not remove the experience of someone trying and failing to find it.

Mira wrote her mother's name on a fresh page of the atlas. Beneath it she wrote the names from the journal, including Elian Vey. She did not yet know what they would mean.

They were not a spell that could return a city or a verdict that could condemn the Council. They were people her mother had thought worth writing down. For tonight that was enough to make the paper feel steadier in her hands.

As they left the library, Cael paused at the door. He looked back at the empty chair and the third cup. The tea had vanished from it, but the cup was warm. Renne refused to look surprised.

She simply asked Mira to bring the journal back when she was done, because an archive depended on people keeping ordinary promises even when the world was doing extraordinary things.

**Mira:** I'll bring it back.

Renne: I believe you. And if you can't, leave someone a note telling them where to look.

Cael shut the door with care. Somewhere beneath their feet the lens continued its enormous unfinished calculation. For the first time that night, Mira did not imagine the machine as a monster with a single heart.

She imagined it as a room full of people who had forgotten how to ask permission, with one empty chair waiting for the person who might teach them to begin.

## Atlas Wakes

The atlas opened of its own accord. Its brass clasps lifted like hands recoiling from hot iron. Across the blank region where Bellwether belonged, pale lettering gathered and dissolved: a street, a garden, the beginning of a person's surname.

The ink never completed a word. Each attempt left a soft sound in the room, like a match being struck in water.

**Cael:** Is that normal?

**Mira:** Until this month the book required someone to turn its pages. My definition of normal has been changing.

The blue spark she had trapped beneath her finger emerged from the paper. It floated toward the suspended globe, and the globe answered with a second spark, then a third. Six of them arranged themselves into a constellation Mira had never seen.

She knew every constellation in the sky. She had also, until very recently, known every one in the atlas.

From the eastern wing came the sound of a door opening under enormous pressure. It was followed by a woman's voice, thin and distant, reciting the longitude of a city that no longer existed. Mira knew the voice.

She had learned to name the stars by following its rhythm from one line to the next. It belonged to her mother, who had died the night her father disappeared.

**Mira:** That voice has been buried for seventeen years.

**Cael:** Then perhaps the grave isn't where we think it is.

Mira closed her eyes. She saw her mother in the kitchen, sprinkling salt on a window ledge to make the weather change. She could not remember whether the salt had worked. She had forgotten the answer long before the city began vanishing.

She wondered how much of a life could be removed without leaving a blank space anyone would think to investigate.

The Council of Cartographers had forbidden the eastern wing after the catastrophe in which her parents disappeared.

Their proclamation was written in language that looked impressive and meant nothing: for the continuity of public navigation, certain navigational mechanisms are to remain undisturbed until such time as they cease to pose a navigational concern. Mira had been seven.

She could now translate the proclamation: we are afraid, and we would rather you be afraid of our authority than of what frightens us.

**Cael:** There are two ways to get to the east wing. An official staircase guarded by people who collect salaries, and the old maintenance route above the orrery. I have experience with roofs.

**Mira:** The atlas has a third path. It connects places by memory instead of distance. The last time I used it, I ended up somewhere I did not recognize and spent three days forgetting why I had gone.

**Cael:** And the safest route?

**Mira:** Sit here until Bellwether becomes a polite gap in somebody else's story.

They both looked down at the book. A small golden circle formed where the southern harbor should have been. As it widened, Mira saw fragments of a street beneath its surface. There was a boy with a red scarf.

There was a dog sleeping in sunlight. There was a shadow on a wall shaped like a woman holding an open umbrella.

They had an evening, perhaps two, before the erased region reached the observatory itself. Mira recognized the way the map pulsed: each loss was a rehearsal for a greater one. The unseen hand would keep drawing circles until no name remained to contradict it.

*Choice: east route*

## East Route

Mira led Cael through the maintenance ladders where her father had hidden a child's height marks among the measurements of the great lenses. She had not visited the place since she was fifteen.

The marks were still there, tracing her growth in pencil from the height of a brass weight to the height of a young astronomer who had begun to be angry all the time.

Above them a machine was turning without touching any of its supports. Its concentric rings moved in contrary directions, one carrying a map of stars, one carrying the words people had given those stars, one carrying only empty slots.

The empty slots were more numerous than the stars. Mira climbed onto the walkway and listened through the metal with the same care she had once given her mother's footsteps on the stairs.

**Cael:** You seem to have excellent reasons for not having told the Council about this.

**Mira:** The Council invented half those reasons.

Behind the third lens she found a ledger stitched with blue thread. It recorded the beginning of the erasure as a series of intentional tests: one deserted path, one abandoned farmhouse, one settlement of thirteen families. Each test had been described as painless.

The final page contained her father's signature and a note in a different hand: unexpected persistence of absence; consider wider application.

**Mira:** They didn't stop because it was dangerous. They stopped because the danger was too small to be useful.

She ripped the ledger free of its wire binding. They left the wing with proof that a catastrophe had not merely occurred but had been prepared. The names of thirteen families echoed inside the book like a quiet crowd waiting to be called.

## Keepers Of The Weather

The route back to the observatory crossed a shelf of cloud where the wind sounded like a hundred people whispering through their teeth. Mira had known the shelf from maps since childhood.

On paper it was a pale strip between two safe crossings, with a warning that travelers should take the northern path during a storm.

In reality it was a place of torn banners, old rope bridges, and bells strung between poles so far apart that no hand could have rung them. Travelers listened to the bells to determine where the air remained solid enough to carry their weight.

Cael noticed first that one of the bells had stopped ringing. He took the telescope from his belt and searched the horizon. Far out along a line of broken stone stood a small weather station, its roof sloping away toward the cloudbank.

A signal lamp flashed from a window. Twice, then once, then twice again. The sequence meant distress. The nearest safe pier was almost an hour away, but the old service path ran beneath the station.

It was a narrow bridge of brass grating, worn smooth by generations of maintenance crews who had crossed it carrying more confidence than equipment.

**Mira:** We should send word. Someone will have a rescue line.

**Cael:** Someone may have had a rescue line when the station was built. Look at the posts. The cables have been cut and rewound.

Mira looked again. The rope was weathered on one side and new on the other, a repair made by a person who had wanted it to pass an inspection without doing the work.

Cael tied his own climbing cord around the nearest post, tested it twice, and gave her the free end. He did not say the path was safe. He asked whether she knew how to belay a rope. She said she knew the theory.

He gave her a brief lesson that involved more practical advice and less reverence for the theory than she was accustomed to hearing.

They crossed one at a time. The clouds below the grating moved in the wrong direction, rising toward them as if the sky were breathing. At the midpoint the bell nearest the station began to ring despite the absence of wind.

A figure appeared in the window and waved both arms. It was a girl of perhaps twelve, wearing a weather observer's coat much too large for her. The sleeves had been rolled up to the elbows.

She shouted that her supervisor had gone down to the engine room and not returned. The engine had stopped at dawn. Since then, the station had been sliding away from the rest of the world.

Inside, a kettle rattled on a cold stove. The wall charts had become transparent in places. A calendar showed yesterday's date beneath tomorrow's weather forecast, but the forecast contained names rather than descriptions of cloud.

The girl's own name was written in a row of careful capital letters around the doorframe, repeated so often that the ink darkened the wood. She said her name was Tessa.

She had begun writing it after her supervisor disappeared, because she was afraid the station would forget her next.

**Mira:** How long have you been alone?

Tessa: I don't know. The clocks disagree. One says three hours. The other stopped the day my mother died. I don't remember putting that clock there.

Cael knelt by the stalled engine and opened the inspection plate. It was not an engine in the usual sense. It measured wind pressure, yes, but beneath the ordinary gears lay a ring of blue glass with the Council's eight-pointed mark.

Someone had attached the weather station to the same network that was swallowing Bellwether. Its power ran through the station's old instruments, using the weather readings to conceal an accounting system that recorded the names of people passing through the cloud shelf.

The supervisor had noticed the alteration. His notebook lay beside the plate, its last entry written with such force that the pen had torn the page: THE WIND IS ONLY THE PART THEY LET US MEASURE.

Beneath it, a column of names ended with Tessa's. A small circle had been drawn around her name, and the next line was blank.

**Mira:** He came down here to stop the machine from taking the next person. He left the chart for whoever followed him.

**Cael:** Can you stop it?

**Mira:** I can stop it making this station part of the agreement. I don't know whether the station will remain connected to the sky afterward.

Tessa had been listening from the doorway. She asked whether Mira's work would bring the supervisor back. Mira said no. Then, because the first answer had been cruelly small, she said she did not know what happened to a person taken by the network.

She would not promise something that the machine had already taught her to fear.

They removed the blue-glass ring with a pair of rusted pliers and a piece of insulated wire. The moment it came free, every bell along the shelf sounded at once. The station lurched.

A crack appeared across the window and widened into a line of white light. Outside, the grating bridge began to come apart at the joints. Cael shouted for Tessa to take the rope and run. She refused until Mira picked up the supervisor's notebook.

The refusal cost them a few precious seconds. It also taught Mira something about the girl she would not have learned from a chart of her likely survival.

They reached the bridge as the station slid away. Cael crossed first and secured the rope to the far post. Mira tied the notebook to Tessa's coat and sent her after him. Then the grating beneath Mira's feet twisted.

She lost her footing and struck the rail with her shoulder. Her atlas opened in the wind.

For one impossible moment its pages held the scattered pieces of the bridge in the air as though a picture of a place could persuade the place not to fall. Cael caught her wrist and pulled.

She landed on the safe side as the station vanished into cloud.

They watched the torn roof go. There was no explosion, only the soft noise of a book closing. Tessa's name remained legible on the notebook. The girl's hands shook so violently that she could not turn its pages.

Mira wrapped her own coat around her and did not ask whether she was brave. Tessa had been brave. She was also cold, frightened, and furious with the world. Calling her brave would not have made any of those things disappear.

The supervisor's final diagram showed three stations marked as auxiliary lenses. The locations formed a triangle around Bellwether, with the observatory at one point and AsterVale at another. The third had no label. Mira copied its coordinates into the atlas.

She did not yet know where the point lay, but it meant that the erasure was not merely spreading. Someone had given it a shape.

At the safe pier, Tessa asked them to write the supervisor's name on a slip of paper. She said she wanted to carry it separately from the notebook in case the ink disappeared. Mira helped her form the letters. The name was Tomas Rell.

She wrote it once, then again. Cael suggested they tie the slips to different places, the way sailors tie spare lines in separate lockers so one flood cannot take them all.

Tessa gave one to Mira, one to Cael, and kept the third tucked beneath her collar.

The pier's caretaker arrived with blankets and hot soup. She knew Tessa's coat but not Tessa, which frightened the girl until the woman explained that all apprentice observers wore the same faded green. They went inside to eat.

Mira stood for a moment at the window and watched the empty cloud shelf. Its bells were ringing again, but without the station they sounded like a warning that had no one left to warn.

**Cael:** We cannot save every station along the route.

**Mira:** I know.

**Cael:** Then don't let the ones we cannot save become a reason not to remember this one.

She held the slip of paper between two fingers. The name TOMAS RELL was still there. They returned to the observatory carrying one more piece of evidence and one more person whose future could not be reduced to a number in a ledger.

## The Garden Where Bells Were Planted

The pier keeper called the place a garden, though Mira could not identify a plant in it.

Every plot was marked by a thin brass stem, every stem by a bell no larger than a plum, and every bell had been packed with soil instead of a clapper.

The stems stood inside low walls of blue stone, arranged not in rows but in a spiral that opened toward the cloud shelf. Someone had left a watering can beside the gate. Its copper spout was polished from use.

The ground was damp, although no rain had touched the pier since the weather station disappeared.

Tessa stopped walking. Her coat still smelled faintly of engine oil and the pier keeper's soup. She made the stiff, protective movement of a child who expected adults to decide something important without explaining it to her.

Mira understood the movement immediately and disliked recognizing it. She had spent the years after her parents vanished practicing the same posture under the Council's long tables.

Tessa: This isn't on the station maps.

**Cael:** Is it on any map?

Mira unfolded the atlas. The plot was represented by a pale oval, without a name. She turned the book to the light and discovered that the oval was not blank. Its surface held shallow depressions, one for every bell in the garden.

The marks looked as if someone had pressed a pen into the paper from the other side, leaving an indentation without ink. She could have taken a rubbing of the page.

Instead she closed the book and asked Tessa whether she wanted to look inside.

The girl considered the gate, then the cloud beyond it. She said she wanted to know where the bells came from, but only if they could leave when she asked. Mira repeated the condition aloud.

Cael unlatched the gate, held it open, and waited for Tessa to walk through first. It was a small ceremony, absurdly formal for an abandoned garden. It seemed to matter to Tessa more than the garden itself.

At the center stood a stone table engraved with dates. There were no names beside them, only a succession of weather symbols: an ordinary sun, a cloud, a brief shower, a gale, and something like a hand drawn over an empty square.

Mira recognized the hand as an old navigators' sign for a location deliberately withheld from the public charts. The symbol had fallen out of use after the catastrophe. The Council's modern maps omitted it entirely.

That was one way to erase a place: first make it unmentionable, then declare it had never been necessary to mention.

Cael found a folded leaflet under the watering can. Its paper had been made from the coarse reeds that grew on the lower islands. The first line read INSTRUCTIONS FOR THE CULTIVATION OF TOMORROW.

Below it, in handwriting too untidy for a Council clerk, someone had written: If a person is lost to the boundary, record a thing they did without being asked. Do not record the manner of their disappearance.

Do not record the names of those who ordered it. Those belong in other books, and there must be other books. Here we keep what the person gave freely.

The next page contained the account of a woman who repaired shoes at the harbor, charging people who could afford a new pair and never charging children. Another described a cooper who left his workshop door unlocked on storm nights.

The descriptions were precise in the way ordinary gratitude is precise. The cooper had once apologized to a stranger for snapping at him because the wood would not bend.

The shoemaker's daughter said her mother sang in the wrong key but never missed the beginning of a song. Their voices had been caught not in portraits or declarations, but in tiny judgments only the living would have made.

Tessa: Why plant bells for them?

**Cael:** Maybe the bells remember their voices.

**Mira:** Or maybe someone wanted a place where remembering meant doing a task. If you come back to water the soil, you have to decide that the story matters again.

They walked the spiral slowly. Some of the bells were new and untarnished. Others had turned green, their surfaces rough with years of salt wind. Many were empty.

In one plot a bell had been removed entirely, leaving a small round indentation in the earth. Beside it lay the broken wooden handle of a trowel. Mira knelt to inspect the gap. The soil there was different.

It contained flecks of powdered blue glass, the same material used to carry the Council's binding through the weather stations.

A person had been taken from this garden after the garden had been built. The fact changed its meaning. The plot was not merely a memorial to losses in the past. It was a place being watched, censored, and sometimes undone.

Someone who believed forgetting necessary had decided that even the harmless remembrance of a shoemaker was dangerous. Mira looked at the spiral and understood that the original gardeners must have kept coming despite the possibility that every visit would be observed.

Under one of the bells Tessa found a card tied with red thread. Its inscription said TOMAS RELL. The girl read the name once, then held the card against the slip she carried inside her collar. The letters matched.

The card was dated four years earlier, long before the supervisor had disappeared that morning. For a moment none of them spoke. The garden had anticipated a loss that had not yet happened. Or the machine had made ordinary chronological language unreliable.

Mira felt the usual temptation to treat the contradiction as a puzzle, something that would be solved once she found the correct equation.

Tessa: He wasn't a ghost when I knew him. He ate pickled onions on toast. He complained about the weather even when he invented the forecast. He would have said this was a ridiculous place for his name.

**Mira:** Then that belongs on the card too.

Tessa looked at her with deep suspicion. She asked whether writing the truth would make him less important, because it was not a beautiful truth. Mira shook her head.

The information that someone had annoyed her was better evidence of his life than a hundred flawless sentences.

Tessa took the pencil from behind Mira's ear and wrote beneath the name: TOMAS RELL ATE TOO MANY ONIONS AND HE WAS KIND WHEN PEOPLE WERE AFRAID. She made the lettering large enough to fill the card.

Then she tied it back with a knot that would be easy to undo.

A bell sounded behind them. It came from the empty plot and produced a noise like a teaspoon against a glass. Cael turned, but there was no wind. The bell sounded again.

Inside the indentation where the missing bell had stood, blue glass began to rise from the soil in a thin column. It was forming letters. Mira read the first few before they dissolved: ACCESS REQUESTED BY THE PRIMARY ARCHIVE.

Someone, somewhere, had asked the machine for this garden's list of remembered people. The request had not yet been granted. In the space between the bells, the soil trembled as though every buried piece of glass were trying to decide whether to answer.

**Cael:** If they have found this place, we cannot stay here.

**Mira:** We can take the cards.

Tessa: But then it won't be a garden.

The girl's objection stopped Mira more effectively than the sound. The cards could be copied. Their information could be hidden in a pocket and carried away. But the gardeners had built a public place, and the publicness was part of the remembrance.

To take every card to safety would make the garden into another secret archive, available to the people who already controlled the maps. Mira had learned the danger of a record being kept by only one custodian.

She had not expected to learn it from someone with soot still beneath her fingernails.

They searched the table for a way to stop the request. The engravings on its surface were not dates after all. They were schedules.

Every weather symbol corresponded to a recurring interval when the machine checked the garden and reported its contents to the Council. It had been doing so for years.

The gardeners had adapted by planting names in soil where official instruments could read only the metallic bell stems. The glass fragments were the remnants of searches the machine had already attempted.

Each time the demand returned, the gardeners broke its instruments and mixed the pieces into the earth.

Cael laughed once, without amusement. He admired the stubbornness of a system that had resisted a colossal machine using nothing more sophisticated than gardening tools. Mira lifted the watering can.

There was a small brass valve inside its spout, the kind used to divert pressure from a meteorological probe. Someone had adapted the can to flood the search mechanism whenever it activated. The water was not water alone.

It held a fine suspension of iron particles that would obscure the machine's magnetic reading long enough for a human being to remove the fragments.

They had no guarantee the device would still work. Cael filled the can from the barrel near the gate. Mira pried open the little valve, turned the spout toward the glowing glass, and poured. The stream struck the column.

It blazed blue, then turned the color of milk, and fell into the soil with a hiss. Several bells answered with gentle notes. The garden seemed suddenly less like a memorial and more like a group of people coughing after an interruption at dinner.

Tessa walked the spiral again. She watered each plot until the can was empty, not because any of them thought the bells could grow, but because the gesture had been explained to her and she had chosen to complete it.

When she reached the gap she stopped. She asked whether she could put a new bell there. Mira found one in a cupboard beneath the table, beside a box of blank cards. The bell was plain copper, without a Council mark.

Tessa set it in the soil and wrote a card for the station supervisor. She made a second card and put it in the cupboard for whoever arrived next.

Under the final schedule mark Mira found a sequence of three numbers scratched into the stone. They matched the coordinates of the unlabeled third weather station. The garden had never been merely sentimental.

Its caretakers had used these bells to tell one another where the network could be interrupted. A simple human tradition had concealed an intelligence system in plain sight.

At the gate Cael noticed that one bell had not sounded. It hung near the path with its mouth pointing toward the empty cloud shelf. The inscription beneath it had been deliberately rubbed away, leaving only two letters at the far edge: VE.

He touched the bell and drew his hand back as if burned. Mira asked what was wrong. Cael said he had been here before. He remembered arriving in rain, carrying a parcel wrapped in oilcloth.

He remembered someone calling him by a name he no longer used. He remembered refusing to put the parcel down because the person waiting for it had not yet reached the gate.

Mira did not ask him to finish the memory. She had seen the strain in his face when he tried to force the past into an orderly narrative. Instead she asked what kind of rain it had been. He thought for a long time.

Then he said it had been warm, with a smell of apples from the lower orchards. It had fallen sideways. The bell had rung without a clapper.

The person he was waiting for had been a woman, and she was laughing at something he had said. That was all he could retrieve. Mira wrote the weather down exactly as he described it.

Not proof of an identity, not a diagnosis of loss; just weather.

The caretaker came to fetch Tessa. The girl gave Mira a paper bell she had folded from a page of the supervisor's notebook. It made no noise. Tessa said that was why she liked it.

It would not go off while she was trying to sleep. She promised to return to the garden when the roads were safe enough, and she made Mira promise to tell her if they were not.

They did not promise that the machine would be defeated. There was too much between them and the machine for a promise of that kind to mean anything.

They walked back toward the observatory. Along the pier the bells continued their small, uneven conversation.

Mira carried one card, a scrap of weather written in Cael's handwriting, and the knowledge that a place could contain a whole argument about who had the right to remember. She had spent her life thinking of maps as descriptions of what existed.

The garden suggested another use. A map could be an invitation to be responsible for something that might otherwise vanish when nobody was looking.

## Second Chapter

By midnight the observatory had acquired a second weather. Outside, thunder rolled along the cloud shelf. Inside, a pale fog gathered on the polished marble and retreated whenever Mira spoke a name that the atlas still recognized.

The six blue sparks circled the globe in the pattern of a sentence she had not learned to read.

She spread everything they had gathered across the main table: the letter from her father, the maintenance ledger or the ticket or the account of the woman in the red coat, and the list of streets she had spent weeks writing out of fear.

Cael sat opposite her with his useless watch between his hands. He had taken its mechanisms apart years ago, he explained, because a watch without hands at least could not accuse him of being late.

**Mira:** Every route gives the same answer in a different language. Somebody made the erasure. The places haven't entirely gone. And the person who kept the machine working knew they were causing harm.

**Cael:** It may be worse. They may have convinced themselves the harm was mercy.

The atlas opened to a new page and displayed an image of the world as it had existed before the northern catastrophe. A coast ran into the distance where no coast could now be seen.

Hundreds of islands hung like scattered petals above the water. A city larger than Bellwether occupied the center of the chart. Beside it stood the word ASTERVALE, written in the old script and surrounded by twelve compass roses.

Mira had heard the name only in a children's song, in which it rhymed with 'never fail.' There were no songs in the library that actually mentioned it.

She opened the index. Someone had sewn the A pages shut. She worked the thread loose with a knife.

Beneath it she found a letter her father had never sent to the Council: What we call an empty sky is a city we have chosen not to remember. AsterVale did not fall. It was exchanged.

If the exchange is reversed without settling the debt, the breach will reopen and all that stands above the clouds will descend into it.

**Mira:** He is describing an agreement. With whom?

**Cael:** The sky, perhaps. It is full of people who insist on negotiating with the weather.

They searched the next pages. The account was not a natural history but the minutes of a council meeting. Seventeen years earlier, when the celestial engine had failed, the astronomers learned that a white storm would consume the island nations.

An ancient instrument could prevent the storm by removing its path from the map of the world. Cities, once excluded from the storm's course, would survive as unremembered territories suspended in an invisible region beside the ordinary sky.

**Cael:** They called it a rescue.

**Mira:** A city cannot consent to a rescue in which it is not allowed to exist.

But the account did not end there. The removal had saved those cities, in a limited sense. The storm had passed over the blank places without striking them. The people inside were still alive, according to the last message received through the eastern lens.

They had no memory of the world outside, and the outside world had no memory of them. They had survived by agreeing to be unfindable.

**Mira:** Why is Bellwether being erased now? The storm passed long ago.

A name flashed across the page: THE TOLL. The instrument had continued to take one memory from the outer world each year as payment for holding the other cities safe. The payment grew.

It had begun with a child's forgotten face, a lost song, a name no longer attached to a street. Now it demanded cities.

When the book reached its own maker's name, the debt would close and everything the instrument held apart would collapse into one another.

The clock struck twelve. Mira heard the first glass pane in the western corridor fracture. From the atlas came the sound of a thousand small bells ringing under water. A line of coastline disappeared.

Above the cloudline, the stars were briefly arranged into the shape of a human hand.

**Cael:** How long?

**Mira:** Until dawn. Perhaps less. My father left a warning about the eastern lens, but the only way to reach AsterVale may be to open it.

Cael picked up the atlas as if it might bite him. It was heavier than the letter. His mouth tightened in a way Mira recognized from people who had decided to say something painful before they could change their minds.

**Cael:** I think I've been there. Not in my current life. In the part of my life that keeps being edited. I know the shape of that bridge. I know where the bakery stands.

**Cael:** And every time I try to remember who took me across it, I lose the beginning of a different memory.

**Mira:** Did you carry letters between the outside and the hidden cities?

**Cael:** I carried messages people were told could not be delivered. I was very good at it. I think someone made me forget the recipients so that I would keep delivering.

Mira wanted to insist he was mistaken. Instead she asked him to show her his palms. On the inside of his right wrist was the faint scar of an eight-pointed compass rose, the same mark her father had used on his letters.

It was not tattooed. It had been burned into the skin by something that had held him against an iron plate.

The first time she had met him, his name was the one thing she had trusted. Now it felt like a bridge that might have been built from both shores by different people.

## Suspect Courier

Mira held the burned compass toward the lamp. He flinched when she did, a reflex too old to be performed convincingly.

Her father's letter had arrived in his hands; an ancient mark lay on his skin; and the hidden city seemed to have preserved fragments of his life. The coincidences had begun to resemble a structure.

**Mira:** You brought me a confession from a man you say you've never met. You have the seal of his work on your wrist. Either you are lying, or somebody designed you to be the perfect messenger to a person they wanted to manipulate.

**Cael:** Those are not the only possibilities.

**Mira:** They are the ones we have time to investigate.

He told her about the dreams. A corridor of polished copper, a woman humming into a glass bowl, a small child who held his hand and called him brother.

The dreams always ended with the same command: when the sky opens, do not let anyone close it again. He had hidden them because he feared they were instructions planted in him, not memories of his own.

Mira found she could be angry with him and still believe his fear was genuine. The distinction did not make her comfortable.

It made her more determined to find out who had the power to make one person doubt the contents of his own mind.

## The Hour That Went Missing

The first symptom was the missing hour on the clock. Mira glanced at the long hand and saw it move backward from eleven to ten without passing through the intervening minutes. She checked the small clock in the stairwell. It agreed with the first.

On the wall near the library, a third clock had stopped entirely, its hands caught between numbers that looked as if they were being erased one at a time. The observatory had begun changing its own record of time.

She looked at Cael. He was standing beside the eastern corridor with a confused expression, the kind a person wore when entering a room to find an object and discovering that the room was not the one expected.

He asked her why they had come upstairs. She answered that they were searching for the truth about the first agreement. He nodded. A moment later he asked again.

**Mira:** What do you remember?

**Cael:** We came through the west door. You had an instrument in your hand. There was a letter. After that ... I know what we found, but I can't remember how we found it.

Mira showed him the ledger, the railway ticket, or the notes from the coast. He read them carefully, then said the names of the places aloud. The facts returned more quickly than the feelings.

He could describe a street he had walked along but not the smell of the rain. He could remember warning Mira about the passage but not what had frightened him. The machine was not removing knowledge at random.

It seemed to be taking the memories most likely to make him return to the people he had lost.

A loud crack sounded above them. The clock nearest the stair fell from its bracket and broke on the floor. Mira knelt among the pieces and found that the face was made from two layers of glass.

Between them was a thin strip of blue paper covered in names. Some had been crossed out. Others were still being written by an invisible hand. The last name was Cael Vey.

Cael watched her read it. He did not ask whether it was his name. He already knew. He asked how long the machine had been writing.

**Mira:** Long enough to make the clocks into part of its records. We have to interrupt the process.

**Cael:** Can you do it without breaking every clock in the building?

**Mira:** I can try. The clocks have survived worse experiments than mine.

They found the master regulator in a room behind the star library. It was a brass cabinet connected by fine wires to every timekeeping instrument in the observatory. A label on the door instructed operators not to open it during an active continuity event.

Mira examined the label and found that the word continuity had been stamped over an older word. The older word was correction.

Someone had taken a device built to synchronize clocks and given it authority over the order in which memories were permitted to exist.

Inside, a wheel turned against a spring that should have held it in place. A sequence of blue sparks traveled along the wires, each one carrying a name toward the eastern lens.

Mira took a pair of insulated pliers and cut the wire marked with the Council's seal. The sparks stopped.

Then the master clock began to tick in two directions at once, a rapid clicking that filled the room with the sound of a hundred small insects.

Cael pressed his hand against the cabinet. The scar on his wrist answered with a faint light. The wheel slowed. Mira understood that the system was using him as a reference point.

His body held a memory of the machine's first calibration, even if his mind had lost the circumstances. The idea made her angry enough that she almost pulled his hand away.

But the wheel was slowing, and Cael was looking at her with the determination of someone who had chosen to stay in the room even after learning how he was being used.

**Mira:** You can stop. I can find another way.

**Cael:** I know. Keep working.

She reached the regulating spring and loosened it one notch. The wheel shifted into alignment. The clocks fell silent. In the corridor, the hands of the broken clock stopped moving. Cael took his hand away and stared at his palm.

He said he could remember an evening on a ship when someone had told him that he would have to choose between keeping a person safe and letting that person travel without him. He did not know whom the choice concerned.

He remembered that he had been afraid to answer.

Mira closed the cabinet. The machine would resume its attempt to synchronize the memories if the Council ordered it. She could not destroy the whole network from this room.

But she had given Cael a little time in which the names would remain where he could find them.

They sat on the stairs beside the broken clock while the observatory settled. Mira took out her ledger and asked him to tell her what he still remembered.

He began with the practical things: how to tie a knot, how to read a cloud shelf, how to repair a torn sail with thread borrowed from a coat. Then he told her about the things he could not name: a woman laughing on

a wet deck, a room with two cups, a child who had once stolen a brass whistle and refused to admit it, a song sung so softly that he could hear only the spaces between the words.

Mira wrote down the fragments and handed the ledger to him. He read the notes twice, as though hoping the act would make the memories return. She offered to keep a second copy in the library. He thanked her.

The distance between them had not disappeared, but it had become a distance both could measure without pretending it was empty.

At last Cael stood. He asked whether she still wanted to enter the eastern corridor. She said yes. He looked at the damaged clock and smiled faintly. He suggested they go before it remembered how to steal another hour.

Mira laughed, then stopped because the laugh sounded so ordinary in a place where almost nothing else did. They took the evidence and went upstairs.

## Ledger Of Absences

The observatory's night watch had been abolished years earlier, but its bells still rang the rounds.

Mira and Cael heard them as they crossed the empty central gallery: one for the lens chamber, two for the librarians' stair, three for the west door, four for the room in which damaged maps were kept under glass.

Between the second and third bells came a pause that had not existed the previous week. Mira could remember the watchman who had complained about it. She could not remember his name.

The missing interval made her feel that the building itself was learning to forget.

They carried the evidence from the night's search upstairs. A ledger, a railway ticket, or an account from a stranger on the cloud coast: each piece weighed almost nothing and refused to lie still on the table.

The atlas drew them toward itself when Mira tried to set them aside. The smaller book slipped open to a page of tide measurements recorded seventeen years before the present date.

Every number in the third column had been replaced by the same penciled mark. Mira recognized the shape from her father's handwriting.

It meant a value had been excluded from a calculation because the person doing the calculation did not know how to measure it.

**Mira:** He was not hiding an error. He was hiding the things he could not count.

**Cael:** What if he thought he was being careful?

**Mira:** Then he was careful with the arithmetic and careless with the lives.

Cael offered to make tea. Mira told him there was no tea left. He opened the cupboard and found a tin with enough leaves for two cups.

When she demanded to know how he had found it, he said he had looked where a person afraid of running out would hide it. The explanation irritated her because it was correct.

They carried their cups into the gallery and sat beneath a painted ceiling full of constellations that no longer corresponded to the sky.

Mira's mother had painted one of those constellations herself. At the time the Council's astronomers were too proud to admit that several stars appeared in the same place on different nights. They had argued about faulty lenses, mistaken observations, and the weather.

Her mother had asked what would happen if the stars were moving and the instruments remained still. She was not a Council astronomer. She was the person who cleaned the lenses when the astronomers went home.

Her question had been laughed at until the new measurements came in. Then the Council published a correction without mentioning her.

The stars in the painted ceiling had not moved, but the real stars had. Mira found her mother's initials in the corner, disguised as three small dots. She had passed under them thousands of times.

Tonight the ceiling looked less like a map than a record of who was allowed to name what everyone else could see.

**Cael:** Would she have believed the atlas could erase a city?

**Mira:** She believed people could do terrible things if they had written themselves permission first. The mechanism would have impressed her. The permission would not.

In the archive room, the oldest volumes were bound in red leather whose color had faded to brown. The catalog called them municipal histories. Their pages contained tax appeals, bridge repairs, disputes about fishing rights, and the inventories taken after winter storms.

These were not the records a Council official would display to visiting dignitaries. They were the records of things people had complained about enough to make someone write them down. Mira had once found them dull.

Now she turned page after page as if she were searching for the heartbeat of the world.

The first empty entry concerned a ferry operator who had requested compensation for a broken landing stage. The second described a school where the teacher believed her pupils were being made ill by an unknown substance in the drinking water.

The third was a petition signed by nineteen residents asking that an old woman be allowed to keep her vegetable garden after the Council purchased the land beneath it. All three petitions had been rejected.

None was important enough to appear in the Council's history of the northern catastrophe. Yet their names and places had faded from every subsequent register. Someone had begun testing the machinery long before any city vanished.

Mira wrote the dates in her ledger. She drew a line from the ferry operator to the school, another from the school to the garden, and at the end of the line a circle where Bellwether should have stood.

The first experiments had not taken place in empty spaces. They had taken place where the Council believed nobody would notice the cost.

Mira recognized a signature on the earliest petition: the same blue flourish she had seen beneath her father's maintenance note. He had witnessed the original test and signed not as a technician but as an approving officer.

She copied the signature even though she knew it would appear in her dreams. Knowledge made her angrier than ignorance had. It also made her less able to imagine that returning to ignorance would improve the world.

For a time they worked without speaking. Cael read the petitioners' names aloud while Mira marked the dates. When he came to one that dissolved between his first syllable and his last, he began again.

He pronounced the imperfect surviving sound, then the sound he remembered, then the silence. Mira wrote all three. Neither knew whether the act would matter. The repetition mattered to them.

Cael had learned to repair sails as an apprentice on the upper routes. He told her that a seam did not hold because the thread was strong; it held because a series of small stitches distributed the strain across cloth that would otherwise tear.

A careless repair could look perfect in harbor and split the first time the wind changed. He had once admired the men who could make repairs invisible.

He had since learned to admire those who left a little colored thread so the next crew knew where the weakness had been.

**Mira:** You're telling me to make the repair visible.

**Cael:** I'm telling you that someone has to know where it might break again.

Mira thought of the monument in the eastern corridor, the scratched-out stars, the official history that gave every vanished person a place in a calculation and none in a sentence. The Council's greatest achievement had been to make its repairs invisible.

The people who lived in the repaired world had been forbidden to see the stitches. No wonder the whole thing was coming apart.

They found a drawer labeled RESTITUTION. It held three coins, a key, a child's brass whistle, and a list of people to whom the objects should be returned when it became possible to find them. The oldest entry was ninety-four years old.

Beneath it, in the same careful hand, was a note: NO AMOUNT PAID. The ledger had survived because no clerk knew whether to classify the restitution as complete or pending. Mira put the coins back and took the whistle.

She would carry it until she could find its owner, or someone who knew the owner's name.

As they left, Cael paused before the doors to the forbidden corridor. His scar had begun to glow so faintly that Mira could see it only when he turned his wrist toward the dark. He said he had been here before.

He did not mean the observatory; he meant this particular door, with its brass lock shaped like two hands that would not touch.

In his memory someone had said that the door would open only for a person who had forgotten what lay beyond it. He could not remember whether the voice had been a warning or an invitation.

**Mira:** You don't have to come through.

**Cael:** I have been avoiding a forgotten place for so long that I have almost forgotten why. If you're willing to open it, I would rather know what I was afraid of.

She turned the key. The lock opened without resistance. A damp draft flowed out carrying the smell of a summer street after rain.

For a heartbeat Mira imagined that Bellwether had returned and was waiting just beyond the threshold, ordinary as the day she last saw it.

Then the draft changed, and she smelled old glass and the electricity of an instrument left running long after its makers were gone. They entered together.

## Third Chapter

The forbidden corridor smelled of old rain and electrical dust.

Once, every room in this wing had contained a practical object: an instrument to measure the tilt of a planet, a clock that could mark the passing of clouds, a map that answered questions about roads.

Now each door was marked with a star, and each star had been scratched out. The absence of the stars unsettled Mira more than the locks. Someone had taken care not to leave a symbol by which the rooms might be remembered.

They passed a gallery of bronze busts, each covered by a dark cloth. Mira lifted the corner of one. The face beneath had been worn smooth, as if by centuries of weather, although the sculptor's date was only twenty-three years ago.

A plaque at the base read: FIRST DIRECTOR OF THE HELD CITY PROJECT. The director's name was gone.

**Cael:** It is possible to build a monument without understanding whom you are honoring.

**Mira:** That is what most monuments are.

The corridor narrowed into a passage made of glass. Clouds pressed against the walls.

Their reflections turned Mira and Cael into a procession: two people followed by other versions of themselves, one older, one younger, one carrying a child, one who had never met the other. The reflections moved out of order.

In one pane Cael reached for Mira's hand before she stumbled; in another she was already falling. The future was as restless as the past.

Mira walked faster. She refused to look at the reflection in which her father appeared beside her.

He wore the blue waistcoat he had worn on the day he vanished and was looking not at the lens but at her, with the exhausted tenderness of a man who had spent his last hope badly.

At the end of the passage stood the eastern lens. It was enormous: a clear sphere held in twelve great rings, suspended over a pit that appeared to descend into the night beyond the world. Blue fires burned around its rim.

Across its surface, innumerable names were engraved in letters as small as the grains of an old photograph.

A woman was standing on the opposite side of the sphere. Her coat was the color of fog, her hair white, her body indistinct at the edges.

Mira knew her from a picture in the kitchen; in the picture she was holding a blue bowl and laughing because something beyond the frame had made her drop a spoon.

**Mira:** Mother?

The woman did not move. Her voice came from the glass, not her throat.

Voice: The lens is not a door. It is an agreement made visible. If you open it without learning the terms, the city you love will become the price of the city you save.

Mira stepped forward. Her hand passed through a patch of cold light. The figure turned into tiny stars, then reassembled at the far side of the lens, nearer than before.

**Mira:** Are you alive?

Voice: There are questions a person may answer only by choosing what they mean by the word. I am what the lens remembered of your mother. I am also a message she left in case you came here before the hour of the closing.

**Mira:** Did she leave me anything else?

Voice: She left you the belief that a daughter might become wiser than the people who were meant to keep her safe.

The words were terrible in their smallness. Mira had prepared for a warning, a confession, perhaps an indictment. She had not prepared to be spoken to as a daughter by a shape that was almost her mother.

She knew the difference between a memory and a person. It did not lessen her wish to be held.

**Cael:** We have not come to break the lens. We want to know what was promised, and to whom.

Voice: Then you must read the three records. The First contains the contract. The Second contains the names the Council removed from it. The Third contains the witness who could make the contract binding again.

You cannot bring all three records through the passage intact. It must be decided which loss you are willing to accept.

Behind the woman, three doors opened onto impossible spaces: a room of burning ledgers, a garden where every flower carried a name, and an observatory seen from beneath an ocean. Mira understood that the choices represented different kinds of evidence.

She also understood that every choice would leave something unknown.

*Choice: record contract*

## Record Contract

The burning room was not hot. Each book flamed with a color like pale green glass, and each page burned only while someone read it.

Mira opened the largest volume and found the first signatures: the Council of Cartographers, the chief astronomer, and the Keeper of the Threshold, who had no hand with which to write.

Under the signatures was a clause no one had ever quoted publicly: the instrument shall preserve the chosen cities beyond the reach of the storm, while collecting from the world a proportionate remembrance of all it has spared.

There was a proviso. The debt could be forgiven if the people of the hidden cities themselves chose to reopen the passage, accepting the return of the storm with it. No outside authority was permitted to make that choice on their behalf.

The Council had hidden this clause. They had continued paying in the names of other people rather than offering the rescued cities a chance to decide.

**Cael:** They knew there was a lawful way out.

**Mira:** They were not willing to let the people whose lives were affected have the final word.

The book closed as the fire reached its binding. Mira carried a single charred page back to the lens, the condition written on it in brass-colored ink. The other doors were gone.

She had gained a right and lost the names of those who might exercise it.

## The School Of Other Names

The passage beyond the records chamber opened onto a garden in which every tree carried a name. The names were written in silver on the bark, not carved into it, and they changed as the leaves moved. Some belonged to people Mira had met.

Others were unfamiliar. A few appeared to be the names of streets, storms, and small villages.

The garden had no visible wall, but whenever Mira walked toward the horizon she found herself returning to the same stone path beneath a row of pale flowering trees.

At the center stood a schoolhouse with an open door. Inside, children were sitting at long wooden desks, copying words from a board. The teacher was an elderly woman whose hair had gone entirely white.

She did not seem surprised when Mira and Cael entered. She told them to sit, handed each a sheet of paper, and asked them to write the name of a person they would not wish to forget. Cael took the paper and hesitated.

Mira wrote her mother's name, then her father's, then crossed out neither.

The teacher glanced at the names. She said she had been asked to teach this lesson for so many years that she no longer remembered who had written the curriculum. Every child who entered the room eventually had to leave. Some returned.

Most did not. The names they wrote were stored in a cabinet behind the board. The cabinet was almost full.

**Mira:** What happens when there is no room left?

Teacher: We make another cabinet. That is the only part of the lesson the Council has never tried to prevent.

One child raised a hand. He wanted to know why some names stayed on the board longer than others.

The teacher said the machine could remove an entry from a register but had not yet learned to remove a question from a person who kept asking it. The child seemed dissatisfied with the answer.

He asked why the machine was allowed to make such a rule in the first place. The teacher smiled and told him that was the next lesson.

After class, Mira examined the cabinet. It contained drawers of folded papers, each labeled with a year. The earliest drawers were filled with names written in large, uncertain letters.

Later drawers held names accompanied by descriptions: the person with the red scarf, the woman who made bread, the man who repaired clocks, the neighbor who sang at the window. As the years passed, the descriptions became longer.

The pupils had learned to describe people by the acts that made them important to others, not merely by the spellings that could be removed from a list.

Cael found a paper near the back of the oldest drawer. The handwriting was small and careful. It read: MY SISTER ELIAN, WHO CANNOT WHISTLE BUT ALWAYS PRETENDS SHE CAN. He stared at it for a long time.

The memory of the girl arrived not as a picture but as a sound: a breathy attempt at a tune, followed by laughter from someone who had already forgotten the melody. He pressed the paper against his chest.

**Cael:** I wrote this.

**Mira:** Do you remember when?

**Cael:** No. But I remember trying to teach her. She was furious that I could do it without thinking.

The teacher said the school had once been part of AsterVale. When the boundary closed, the Council had removed the building from the city's official plans because it did not know how to classify a place that existed partly in memory.

The children continued to come. Some arrived as dreams. Others stepped through ordinary doors on their way to school and discovered that they had entered a classroom that did not belong to the day outside.

The teacher had never been certain which children were real. She had decided to teach them all.

Mira sat at an empty desk and examined the exercise book left there. On one page a child had drawn a map of the garden.

Every tree was marked with a small star, and at the edge of the page a path led toward a door labeled WHEN YOU ARE READY. The path did not correspond to any gate in the garden.

It was the kind of map a child might draw to remember a feeling rather than a place. Mira closed the book gently.

The teacher showed them a room behind the school where objects that had lost their owners were kept. There were shoes, toys, broken cups, a bundle of letters, and a small brass bell. The bell had no clapper.

The teacher said it had belonged to a witness who could call people back from the boundary by speaking their names. When the witness disappeared, the bell remained. The Council had asked for it repeatedly.

The teacher refused to surrender it because the school was not required to return every object to the institution that had lost it.

Mira understood that the witness's bell might be connected to the records they had already taken. She asked whether they could borrow it.

The teacher said they could, provided they promised to bring it back when the children had finished learning what it was for. Mira hesitated. She did not want to promise something she might be unable to do.

The teacher nodded, as if the hesitation were the correct answer, and told her that promises could be made with an honest statement of uncertainty.

They took the bell. Its metal was warm. Cael held it in both hands and listened for a sound that did not come. Mira asked whether he could remember his sister's voice. He said he could remember the attempt at whistling, not the words.

It was enough to make him want to keep looking.

At the garden gate, the teacher gave Mira a small packet of seeds. She said they came from a tree whose name had been lost during the first catastrophe. The tree continued to grow. It had not waited for anyone to restore its label.

Mira put the seeds beside the brass whistle and the scraps of evidence in her satchel. The packet was a reminder that not every living thing required a complete history in order to have a future.

The garden path returned them to the eastern corridor. Behind them the schoolhouse door closed gently. Mira could still hear the children repeating names. She had entered the garden believing the records were evidence about a machine.

She left knowing that the people who lived under the machine's rules had been making their own records all along, in the shapes of lessons, gardens, and ordinary acts of care.

## Chamber Of Measures

The three records converged on a room beneath the eastern lens. Its ceiling was made of curved glass, and above the glass something that resembled water moved slowly against gravity. Tiny points of blue light drifted through it.

Mira knew the technical explanation: the chamber was a pressure-balancing cavity connected to the lens. Her father had designed it to demonstrate that a sealed system could hold a force without allowing the force to escape. The resemblance to an ocean was a coincidence.

It frightened her anyway.

The room contained twelve chairs and a long table. Each chair bore the name of a Council minister on a brass plaque. The names were still present; nobody had tried to erase them.

The table was marked with a grid of numbers, one for every place the machine had been ordered to preserve. Mira ran her hand across the grid and found that some squares were warmer than others.

In the warmer squares were tiny paper slips, folded so tightly she had to use the tip of her instrument to open them.

They listed costs: an orchard exchanged for a bridge, a row of houses for a hospital wing, a railway platform for a winter without famine. At the bottom of each slip was a signature.

**Cael:** They made the losses look like payments.

**Mira:** Payments made by people who were never told they owed anything.

She unfolded the oldest slip. It concerned a fishing village whose inhabitants had been moved inland during a storm. The Council's report said the transfer had saved everyone. An annotation beneath the number of survivors described the cost as a minor displacement.

Mira turned the paper over. On the back, in an unfamiliar hand, someone had written that the village's graveyard had remained on the coast, and the families had been forbidden to return to it. The Council counted lives saved.

The families counted the names of their dead. Both numbers were true. Only one had been permitted to govern what happened next.

Cael examined the empty place at the end of the table. There was no chair there, only a circle cut into the floor where something heavy had once rested. The groove was wide enough to hold a person.

A brass rail ran from the circle to the lens above. He recognized the arrangement from the old courier stations, where a message could be sent through a vacuum tube by placing it inside a sealed carrier.

The Council had built a carrier large enough for a human body.

He put one boot on the circle and felt the scar on his wrist grow hot. The glass above them brightened. A voice came from the water, quiet and precise: SUBJECT RECOGNIZED. TRANSIT AUTHORITY INCOMPLETE.

Mira pulled him back before he could step farther in. The circle went dark, but the voice had told them something no ledger contained. Cael had been registered as a traveler whose permission was required for the system to operate.

His forgetting had not made his authority disappear. It had simply made him unable to exercise it.

**Mira:** You were not only a messenger. They made you part of the mechanism.

**Cael:** Then why do I remember only the journeys?

**Mira:** Because someone needed you to keep traveling, not to understand why.

He sat in the nearest chair, a minister's name beneath his hands. Mira wanted to tell him this made him important. She recognized the cruelty of that comfort before she spoke. It meant he had been used.

His place in the machine was not proof that the people who built it valued him. It was proof that they valued what they could make him do.

They found a cabinet of sealed testimony. Each drawer contained a statement given during the first months after the catastrophe. Some witnesses described an extraordinary storm. Others described the smell of bread in a city they could no longer reach.

One person said the boundary had been erected to protect children from a disease; another said the disease had appeared only after the boundary. The statements contradicted one another in details. They agreed about the fear.

The Council's published account had removed the contradictions and all mention of the Council's own uncertainty.

**Mira:** If we take this to the city, they will disagree about what it proves.

**Cael:** They already disagree. The difference is that they might finally be allowed to disagree about the evidence instead of about the rumor of it.

The glass ceiling shuddered. A dark figure appeared beyond the blue water, too distant for them to see its face. It raised one hand. A line of text formed across the lens: THE SYSTEM HAS NEVER CLAIMED TO BE JUST.

The figure lowered its hand. Another line appeared: IT CLAIMS TO PREVENT A WORSE OUTCOME. Mira stared until the letters began to drift apart. It was the defense the Council had offered for every action whose consequences it wished to conceal.

Mira found the date of the last recalculation. It had been made only three days earlier. The Council had not lost control of the machine by accident; someone was still entering revised values while the city disappeared.

The ink on the paper had barely dried. The evidence meant they were not dealing only with the mistakes of the dead. They were confronting people who knew enough to stop and had chosen to continue.

At the far wall hung a framed map of Bellwether made from hundreds of narrow metal strips. Each strip carried the name of a road.

The strips could be moved to simulate the city under different conditions: a flood, a fire, a population increase, a damaged bridge. Mira had studied the model in her childhood and admired its precision.

Now she noticed that no strip represented the people who lived along those roads. The city was modeled as a set of routes through which supplies could pass. It had no room for a person sitting beside a window while waiting for a friend.

She began removing the strips. Cael helped without asking what pattern she intended to make. They laid the metal on the floor in the order of the stories they had heard: the bakery with the blue door, the station whose platform ended in the

sea, the garden with the crooked sticks, the ferry operator who had wanted a safe landing, the woman who had sent a letter through a broken passage. The new arrangement was not a map a navigator could use.

It was a map of reasons people might want to return.

As the last strip fell into place, the light behind the ceiling shifted. The water became clear. For one heartbeat they could see AsterVale beneath it: not an image projected by the lens but a real city observed from an impossible angle.

A person leaned out of a window to call a child home. The call sounded distant and imperfect, and neither Mira nor Cael could understand the name. They listened until the image vanished.

Mira knew she could not carry the whole table, all the testimony, and the metal strips into the chamber above. She chose the few pieces that showed how the calculation had been made and the stories it had excluded.

Cael put the remaining testimony in the center of the table where it would be visible to whoever entered next. He turned every minister's chair toward it. It was a small act of impertinence. Mira thought her mother would have approved.

They climbed the narrow stair toward the storm. Above them, the glass continued to hold its impossible ocean, but the currents had changed direction. The chamber was beginning to respond to a map on which people occupied more space than the machine had intended.

Mira could not decide whether the change meant they were succeeding or whether they had awakened something that had been sleeping for seventeen years. She went upward anyway.

## The Room Beneath The Weather

The staircase from the Chamber of Measures should have ended at the western gallery. Mira had climbed it every week as an apprentice, carrying boxes of brass weights to be calibrated in the room above.

She remembered the exact number of steps, the place where the handrail was loose, the little notch in the plaster left when she dropped a weight and refused to admit it. On the seventeenth step the stair turned downward instead of up.

On the eighteenth it disappeared into water.

The water did not fall into the stairwell. It hung in front of them, occupying the shape of a room, clear and dark as glass. An enormous lens floated at its center. Schools of pale fish circled it without disturbing the surface.

Mira could see a table on the other side, laid with plates of dinner that had never been served. Above the table hung a chandelier, its candles burning underwater. The flame of each candle pointed toward the floor.

**Cael:** Tell me this is another memory.

**Mira:** I don't know what it is.

**Cael:** That's becoming a remarkably common answer from the world's foremost cartographer.

**Mira:** I'm not the world's foremost cartographer.

**Cael:** You've just corrected me about a title at the entrance to a flooded room in a staircase. I think the title is yours.

He took her hand. She could feel the rough seam of his glove against her palm. It was a useful sensation because the room ahead had begun whispering in the voices of people she knew, and the hand reminded her that the person beside

her was real, at least in the way a person could be real to another person standing on a stair that had forgotten which direction led upward.

A figure sat at the table. It was an older woman in a blue shawl, her dark hair bound with a ribbon whose knot Mira remembered tying as a child. The figure lifted a teacup. The water did not enter it.

Mira moved forward before she had made the decision to move, and Cael pulled her back from the surface. His grip was too hard.

She turned on him, angry, and saw that he was looking not at the woman but at a second figure standing behind the chair.

The second figure was Cael himself, younger and thinner, holding the hand of a child in a red coat. He was speaking without sound. The child looked up at him.

His younger face was frightened and determined; he had the expression of someone trying to make the last possible promise before the room in which he stood ceased to exist.

**Cael:** That is Elian. Or it was. I remember the coat. I bought it with the first money I earned carrying letters across the outer islands. She wore it even in summer because the lining had pockets large enough for stones.

**Mira:** The machine is showing us memories.

**Cael:** Then why are they sharing a room? Your mother and my sister were not at the same table. I would remember.

**Mira:** Would you?

The question was crueler than she intended. Cael let go of her hand.

The water trembled in the outline of their fingers, and the people behind it changed places: Mira's mother rose and stood beside the child, while the younger Cael took the empty chair and covered his face. Mira wanted to apologize.

She also wanted to know whether there was any way of asking a person for evidence when the person had been trained by violence to doubt his own recollection.

They stood in silence until the candles began to move. The flames unfastened from their wicks and swam through the water like gold minnows. Each one carried a small sound, a syllable from a name.

Mira recognized some: Renne, Tomas, the baker's granddaughter, the young apprentice who had vanished from the weather station. The flames formed a ring around the lens and stopped. On its surface a sentence appeared: THE SEA HOLDS WHAT THE SKY WAS ASKED TO FORGET.

Mira reached into the water with one finger. It was not wet. It was cold, and beneath the cold was the sensation of being watched by many people who had not agreed to be made into a single witness. She withdrew her hand.

A tiny blue mark remained on her skin, shaped like the coastline of an island she had never visited. The mark glowed once and faded.

The chamber belonged to the Observatory's oldest experiments. Her father had written of a place beneath the weather where failed projections could be kept without altering the living world. Mira had thought he meant a storage room for damaged maps.

She now understood that the phrase had been literal: the machine had stored whole unrealized days in an artificial sea, and the days had not become harmless merely because no one remembered living them.

Cael's younger self stood in the water and approached the surface. He raised a palm. The scar on his wrist shone like the one Cael carried now. The image spoke, and this time they heard it clearly.

Young Cael: The letter was not addressed to the Council. It was addressed to the woman who made the bells. We were supposed to tell the cities the first agreement had a second clause. The Chancellor stopped the delivery.

**Cael:** Did she erase the letter?

Young Cael: She erased the morning in which I delivered it.

The memory bent beneath an unseen current. The child in red vanished. Cael stumbled forward, caught himself on the railing, and looked down at his wrist. A line of blue light crossed the scar. Mira called his name.

He answered, but his voice sounded distant, as though he were already walking inside the memory with the younger man.

Mira remembered the phrase her mother had written in the blue journal: an instrument should help us notice what we have failed to see, not decide for us what is permitted to remain.

If the room was full of memories cut away from the world, its danger was not that they might be false.

Its danger was that the living might become so absorbed in recovering what had been taken that they surrendered the remaining hours in which they could still act.

**Mira:** Cael, look at me.

He did not move. She said his name again. The room echoed it back in different voices, some old, some young, one voice too small to belong to the man standing beside her. Then Mira did something she had not planned.

She began describing the journey they had actually made: the broken weather station, the cups of tea with Renne, the ferry rail he had repaired, the pears they had eaten walking toward the hidden market.

She described the way he had complained about the ferryman's paperwork and how Daro had laughed when the jar proved difficult to open.

Cael's hand loosened on the railing. He turned toward her, still pale.

**Cael:** There was a boy with a cart.

**Mira:** Yes.

**Cael:** His left foot was in a splint. I told him to rest it, and he told me resting wouldn't get the pears delivered.

**Mira:** He was right about one part of that.

**Cael:** You were covered in syrup. I said you looked like a scholar who'd lost an argument to a fruit.

**Mira:** You were not wrong.

He laughed once, shakily. The water receded from the stairs. In the room beyond, the younger Cael stepped away from the surface. The memory did not dissolve.

It remained where it was, waiting for someone who could return to it without abandoning the present to do so.

The woman's blue shawl shifted aside, revealing a ledger beneath the empty plate. Mira could read its heading through the water: UNDELIVERED MORNING, EASTERN LENS, SECOND CLAUSE. She copied the reference number into the atlas.

She did not try to recover the whole day at once. The number would be enough to find the original record if the library survived.

The underwater table began to rise. Its plates turned into circular charts, each one displaying the outline of a possible storm. The first chart showed Bellwether torn from its foundations. The second showed AsterVale caught inside a permanent ring of cloud.

The third showed the two cities joined by a bridge that existed for exactly one afternoon, long enough for families to cross before the sky decided it had been a mistake.

The fourth showed no storm at all, but a blank continent where every road had been made perfectly safe by removing the people who might have traveled it.

Mira examined the charts in turn. The instrument had been built to compare outcomes. She had studied such systems her whole life, convinced that if enough consequences could be displayed, choosing between them would become a matter of technical competence.

Yet every chart required an answer to a question no calculation could resolve: what is a life allowed to risk for itself? The storm on one chart might be a tragedy. The absence of the storm on another might be a captivity.

Neither could be judged by counting only the bodies that remained afterward.

A mechanical bird appeared in the water. It had a small brass chest and wings made from thin sheets of mother-of-pearl. One wing was broken, and every time the bird tried to fly it turned in a circle and collided with a submerged wall.

Cael watched it for several attempts. Then he stepped close to the surface, took a screwdriver from his pocket, and held it out toward the bird. It could not reach the tool. It could only continue circling.

**Cael:** You can't repair everything from outside the room.

**Mira:** No.

**Cael:** But if you go inside, you may become part of the room.

**Mira:** I know.

He placed the screwdriver on the railing. It was a small tool, its wooden handle darkened by use. The machine recognized the act as an offering and pulled the tool gently through the water. The bird landed beside it.

A tiny mechanical arm emerged from the table, examined the screw at the broken wing, and tightened it. On its next attempt the bird rose, uncertainly, into the currents.

It made one complete circle without striking the wall, then flew toward the woman in blue.

Mira felt tears arrive without warning. The bird had been repaired by a mechanism built into the same instrument that had stolen cities. The machine was not evil in the satisfying sense of a villain who could be removed and leave everything else untouched.

It was capable of help. It was capable of harm. Its power had allowed people to believe that the difference no longer needed to be decided in public. That was what had to change.

The woman in the blue shawl turned toward Mira. Her face was not quite the face Mira remembered. Memory had stretched its features and softened them in places; grief had made others too sharp. But the gesture she made was familiar.

She touched two fingers to her forehead, then held them outward toward her daughter, the way she had done when Mira left for school.

**Mira:** Mother?

The water shook. The woman's mouth moved, but the sound emerged from the old bell above the staircase instead of from her lips. It was a single note.

The note carried no sentence, no secret instruction, no final pardon for a father whose mistakes had not been hers to excuse. It carried the sound of someone acknowledging that another person was there.

Mira answered the gesture. She touched two fingers to her forehead and held them toward the room. The woman's figure faded, and the table settled to the bottom of the sea. The broken bird landed on a brass ring beside the lens.

Behind it the four charts remained visible, but their edges were no longer fixed. The currents moved through them, changing the shape of the storm as though the future were again permitted to contain more than one possibility.

The staircase straightened. Where water had hung in the doorway, a narrow flight of stone led upward toward the southern gallery. The storm could be heard through its windows. Mira knew the delay had cost them time they did not possess. She also knew

they had brought something out that the Council had not intended anyone to carry: not an authoritative vision of the past, but a memory returned to two people who had been allowed to speak about it without a clerk deciding which version was official.

Cael retrieved his screwdriver from the railing. It was dry. One of its screws had been polished by the machine, and a small star had been etched into the handle. He turned it between his fingers, puzzled.

**Cael:** Do you think it will remember what I asked it to do?

**Mira:** It remembered how to mend a wing.

**Cael:** Perhaps that is a beginning.

He offered her his arm. She took it, and they climbed together into the storm.

Behind them the room beneath the weather remained where it had always been, full of the days people had been denied and of the possible days they might yet choose to live. Mira did not promise to return and empty it.

She promised herself that if she ever returned, she would bring people who could decide what belonged to them.

The air in the gallery smelled of ozone and old paper. Rain struck the windows from below. A blue circle burned in the center of the marble floor where the Chancellor's seal would soon appear.

Mira set the atlas on a dry bench and checked the reference number written beside her mother's name. It was still there. So was Cael, and the screwdriver, and the ordinary ache in her feet after climbing too many stairs.

There were questions she had not answered. There would be more. She went to meet the woman who had built a government out of the claim that no questions remained.

## An Inventory Of Small Mercies

The submerged chamber released them at the bottom of an ordinary staircase. Water ran from the cuffs of Cael's coat and pooled between the marble tiles. Behind them, the impossible door remained open for a moment, showing the submerged ceiling and its motionless papers.

Then it shut, revealing a wall hung with the official portraits of observatory directors. Their faces looked away from the stairwell with a collective dignity that now seemed less like confidence than a refusal to witness.

Mira carried a small tin box she had recovered from beneath the water. Its lid was stamped with the old seal of the Ministry of Relief. Relief was not a word she had expected to find in that place.

The Council had always represented the first experiment as an act of navigation, a necessary response to a disaster in the upper air.

A ministry devoted to relief suggested there had been another public explanation, one offered to people who needed medicines, shelter, and food rather than a precise account of where their homes had gone.

They found the ministry's former office behind a door whose glass had been painted black. In the center of the room stood a desk with three drawers, none of them locked. There were no weapons, no guards, no hidden machine parts.

Only a ledger of household supplies, a broken scale, and a line of empty bottles with labels still tied to their necks. The air smelled of lavender and old paper. Mira noticed that the bottles were all the same size.

It was the sort of administrative uniformity she might once have admired.

**Cael:** This place doesn't look like a laboratory.

**Mira:** It wasn't. It was where the laboratory sent its consequences.

The ledger listed blankets, hot water, children's shoes, and replacement papers. Each item had a number, a date, and a column for the recipient's signature.

For several pages the signatures were legible: dockworkers, nurses, a pastry maker, a teacher from Bellwether whose lessons had ended the week the city disappeared. Then the signatures grew scarce.

The later entries were marked with a little square, an office shorthand for unable to sign. Some of the people receiving supplies had been too young. Some had lost the use of their hands.

Some were missing from the locations to which the supplies were supposedly delivered.

Mira followed one column with her finger. A shipment of blankets had been dispatched to a school whose address no longer existed. It had been returned three days later, still wrapped, and sent out again under a different place-name.

For seventeen years the ministry had repeated the same procedure, sending supplies to the edges of erasure and recording the failures as problems of distribution. The accounts were perfectly balanced. No blanket was ever counted as lost. No child was ever recorded as cold.

The numbers told a story of exemplary diligence because the numbers had been designed not to ask the important question.

Cael found a sheet of letters wedged behind the last drawer. Each began with the same sentence: We regret that the location named in your request cannot be found on current charts.

The rest changed according to the relationship between the writer and the missing person. A mother had asked where her son's school had gone. A boy had asked why his sister's name no longer appeared in the family register.

An old man had asked for permission to put flowers beside a bridge that no longer crossed anything. The ministry's replies were correct according to the charts. They were devastating according to every other measure of correctness.

**Mira:** They made kindness into a kind of accounting. If the response was properly recorded, they counted the need as answered.

**Cael:** And they could keep saying they were helping, because there was always another form to fill out.

She expected him to be angry. He was angry, but his anger had an almost scholarly patience.

He had carried letters to people who did not want them, delivered notices of eviction, brought medicine too late, and learned that a messenger's responsibility did not always end when the recipient accepted an envelope.

He described the most difficult delivery of his life: a letter containing good news for a family whose father had died the previous morning. He had sat on the doorstep while the daughter decided whether she wanted him to read the letter aloud.

He still remembered that it had concerned a scholarship. The girl had wanted the news, but she had not wanted to hear it from a stranger until someone she loved came home.

**Cael:** A delivery can be right and still arrive at the wrong time. That's why I wait if someone asks me to.

**Mira:** Did the scholarship change anything?

**Cael:** I don't know. I wasn't there for the years afterward. That's the part no messenger gets to claim.

A door on the far side of the office opened with a faint metallic click. They turned. An elderly woman stood in the doorway, holding a basket of empty bottles.

She wore the plain grey apron of a ministry attendant and had a scar under her chin that moved when she swallowed. She regarded the strangers, the open ledger, and the water at their feet without displaying surprise.

She asked whether they wanted the department of ordinary petitions or the department of extraordinary losses. When Mira said she wanted the truth, the woman gave a tired smile and told her that had always been a third department, but nobody had funded it.

Her name was Aven. She had worked there since before Mira was born, first as a clerk and later as a caretaker after the Ministry of Relief formally ceased to exist.

She had stayed because the shelves still contained people's letters and someone had to keep the mice away. The Council paid for the building to remain closed but had never issued an order to destroy its contents.

An absence of authority, Aven said, could sometimes be useful. She knew the circumstances were not the same everywhere. In many offices a destruction order had arrived within hours of the first complaints.

Aven: Do you know why they called it relief? Because the public could bear almost any explanation if it included a plan to help their children. They were frightened, and these people were frightened too. Fear is not an excuse.

But fear was the material the Council worked with.

**Mira:** You knew what the machine was doing?

Aven: No. I knew that some children came back with two birthdays. I knew a woman asked for bread for her husband long after the husband had been pronounced nonexistent. I knew we were not permitted to ask whether either event made sense.

When I understood, the first thing I did was ask for another job. There were no other jobs.

Mira had prepared herself for the bureaucrats of this forgotten office to be villains. Aven complicated the picture without making the harm smaller. She described the bread deliveries she had arranged quietly after office hours, using her own wages.

She also described the afternoon she had signed a report declaring a family absent from its address because refusing to sign would have ended the supplies for three other families. She did not ask Mira to admire the arithmetic.

She said she thought about it every evening and did not know whether the people who suffered from the decision would find her reasons persuasive.

The ledger held several dozen decisions of that kind. Some employees had forged signatures to make sure medicines were released. Others had forged them to conceal that medicines were never distributed.

The same tool could be used to resist the machine or to feed it. Mira was beginning to see why the machine's authors had preferred numbers to testimony.

Numbers could be compared without requiring the people responsible to tell the difference between mercy and cowardice.

Aven opened a cabinet near the window. Inside were children's drawings, folded in bundles and tied with blue string. She explained that families who had not been able to name their lost addresses had sometimes drawn the places instead.

The ministry had kept the pictures because the drawings were not considered evidence. The distinction had saved them. A map drawn by a surveyor could be confiscated.

A child's picture of a kitchen with five chairs, when the family register recognized only four people, could be filed away as a harmless confusion.

The first drawing Mira unfolded showed a yellow house beneath three purple trees. It had no scale, no directional markings, and a sun that occupied half the page. Under the house a child had written THE PLACE WHERE MY BROTHER PUT HIS BOOTS.

The next showed two women crossing a bridge with a dog whose legs extended beyond the paper. The bridge was unmistakably the eastern viaduct of Bellwether. The third showed the observatory, drawn as a crooked tower beside a square of stars.

Mira recognized the clumsy shape of the astronomy instrument at its summit. A small figure with a blue scarf stood beneath it, waving at someone in an upstairs window.

She realized she was holding a record from the night her father disappeared. The date on the back had been entered in a clerk's hand. The name field was blank. Someone had kept the drawing without knowing what they had preserved.

Mira laid it carefully on the desk. She could not identify the child who made it. She could not even be certain the tower was meant to be hers.

But the drawing described a place the charts denied, and she could ask the people who remembered the child whether the picture was true.

Aven: I always thought that one mattered. Not because it is more accurate than the others. Because the child made a window. Most people draw a house from the outside. That child wanted you to know there was someone inside waiting to be seen.

**Cael:** Did the child return?

Aven: I don't know. We never followed the letters after we answered them. We weren't permitted to be curious.

They made an inventory of the cabinet. Aven insisted they distinguish between the original drawings and their copies.

She showed Mira a way to mark the back of each copy with the day it was made, so a future reader could tell whether the original had changed. She also asked that the images not be published without any surviving family's agreement.

A drawing could reveal intimate details about a home. The Council's surveillance had taught her that recovering a memory did not give the recovering institution ownership of it.

Mira located a second copy of the relief ministry's founding charter under the scale. It contained a sentence struck out in red: all beneficiaries of the boundary shall be informed of the losses borne by the outer districts.

The deletion had been approved by Orsol's predecessor. Mira copied the signature as well as the sentence. Public disclosure had once been contemplated. It had been discarded in a room as ordinary as this one.

Aven unlocked the box from the submerged chamber with a small key on a chain around her neck. Mira stared at the key. Aven said the ministry had once maintained a route through the eastern lens to receive petitions from people within the boundary.

The route had been shut after a messenger returned carrying only the names of the people who had sent him. He had forgotten his own. The box contained copies of the last accepted petitions.

Aven had kept the key because she could not bear the thought of a closed door whose lock nobody could ever open again.

The papers inside were not requests for rescue. They were requests to be heard. A school principal wanted the boundary residents to determine their own curriculum. A midwife wanted medicines delivered without a Council official accompanying them.

A group of dockworkers wanted a weekly list of who had been allowed to leave. Their complaints had been sent outside the boundary and recorded as irrelevant because they did not fit the language of emergency relief.

Mira looked at the petitions, then at the neat column of balances in the ledger. The people inside had been asking for a voice while the people outside had been offering them blankets.

Aven gave them the box, but only after they agreed to return its contents or place them in an archive controlled by representatives of the petitioners. Mira promised neither outcome would be decided by the Council alone.

The woman made her write the promise on the back of the inventory. Cael signed as witness. Aven signed beneath them, slowly, as though a signature that had once helped the machinery of silence might become a different kind of act.

At the door, Mira asked Aven why she had stayed. The woman considered the question and answered it as directly as she had answered everything else. She had stayed because she knew where the letters were.

If she left, they might be destroyed by an employee who had never heard the voices behind them. She had also stayed because she was frightened of discovering what her own life would be without the building in which she had made her compromises.

She did not know which reason had mattered more. Mira found the honesty painful and reassuring.

They carried the tin box into the main hall. Outside, the storm was still holding its breath. The observatory's glass roof caught the last of the afternoon light and distributed it across the marble in pale stripes.

Cael made a list of people to contact. Mira made a list of questions they could not answer yet. The two lists did not match. That, for once, seemed like evidence that they were beginning to do the work correctly.

## Fourth Chapter

The storm began at the wrong end of the sky. Lightning rose out of the clouds and vanished into the stars without striking the observatory; thunder arrived before its light. Every window flashed with a different image of the city that had been erased.

In one pane Bellwether was burning. In another it had never been built.

In a third, small children were playing a game of marbles in streets that still existed, oblivious to the fact that their existence was being argued over by people who were not present.

Mira laid her chosen evidence on the floor beneath the eastern lens. The sphere brightened. For the first time she could see beyond the glass: a market, an avenue of cypress trees, and a canal illuminated by a thousand floating candles.

AsterVale was beautiful and entirely ordinary in the ways that mattered. Its people were hurrying home before the weather, losing gloves, finding missing coins, quarreling over the price of fruit. The Council's histories had spoken of it as an abstract quantity, an instrument's burden.

It was impossible to watch the people crossing the square without recognizing how much that description had concealed.

Cael pressed his hand against the glass. His scar answered with a blue pulse, like an artery transmitting light. For an instant the surface became soft. He recoiled, then pressed again.

Mira saw a girl on the other side, perhaps twelve years old, wearing the same blue scarf she had seen in the fragment of Bellwether. She raised her hand to meet his, but the glass held a distance neither of them could measure.

**Cael:** I know her.

**Mira:** From where?

**Cael:** I know the way she frowns when she is trying to remember the name of a bird. I know the first word she ever said, and that it was not a word at all, only a sound we all pretended to understand.

**Cael:** I know she used to draw windows on the inside of boxes so that things put away would not become lonely.

He stepped back. His face had gone pale. The girl on the other side was turning away, called by a woman from somewhere behind the market.

**Cael:** She's my sister. I had a sister.

Mira had seen him smile in the early evening. Now she watched the exact moment a man discovered that the space left by a person was larger than his life.

She reached for him, then stopped when she saw the way his fingers were shaking.

**Cael:** Her name is Elian. It was Elian. I do not know whether it still is.

**Mira:** We will find out.

He looked at her with sudden fear, not for himself but for the promise she had made. It was easy to say they would find someone who had been removed from the world. Such promises could become a new way of being cruel.

An immense shadow crossed the glass. The market disappeared. The Council's seal rose in its place, followed by a figure made of gold lines: Chancellor Orsol, director of the observatory and presiding cartographer of the upper islands.

She had taught Mira mathematics when she was a child. Later, when Mira asked where her father had gone, Orsol answered with the phrase all powerful people use when they have decided a child will not remember the answer: one day you will understand.

Orsol: Mira Vale. You have opened a prohibited instrument and disturbed the agreement that keeps these islands safe. I expected better of you.

**Mira:** I expected the same of you, Chancellor. We seem to be having a disappointing evening.

Orsol: Your father's actions were necessary. He understood what you have not yet learned: a disaster averted is invisible evidence. You cannot point to the people saved from a storm that never struck and demand to see their gratitude.

**Mira:** You can ask them whether they consent to being kept outside the world. You never did.

Orsol: If the hidden cities choose to return, the storm returns with them. The outer islands will fall. Their children will drown in the clouds. This is the weight on the other side of your argument.

**Mira:** Then tell the people the truth and allow them to choose with it.

Orsol: Truth is a luxury when one has the time to debate. We have less than four hours. Close the lens. Give me the atlas. I will renegotiate the toll and delay the closing for another generation.

The offer sounded rational in exactly the way dangerous things often do when someone has carefully removed the part of the problem that cannot be solved.

Another generation meant another century of missing families, erased schools, and mothers setting places for daughters whose names were gone. The Chancellor had lived long enough to believe that postponement was a form of mercy.

**Cael:** If you know the hidden city is alive, why did you tell me that the voices I heard there were dreams?

Orsol looked at him as if seeing an inconvenient detail in an old equation.

Orsol: Because a messenger who remembers where he has been cannot be safely sent back. Your mind was not strong enough to carry the boundary. We spared you the burden.

**Cael:** You took my sister and called it mercy.

Orsol: I kept her alive.

The silence after that was more frightening than the storm. Mira understood that both claims could be true and that the truth of one did not cancel the violence of the other. The atlas was not an ethical machine.

It was a machine in a world where ethical choices had been withheld until they resembled mechanical necessities.

Orsol: I will be at the eastern stair when the last bell sounds. If you are wise, you will be there before me.

The seal collapsed into a circle of blue light. The market returned. Elian was gone.

Cael stood very still beside the glass, and Mira felt how little use it was to be right about a question when someone had just been given back a grief he had not known he possessed.

*Choice: negotiate route*

## Negotiate Route

Mira pulled a chair to the eastern lens and sat down as if she were preparing for a difficult faculty meeting. She took out clean paper, a dry pen, and three empty columns. At the top of the first she wrote THE CITY.

At the top of the second THE OUTER ISLANDS. At the top of the third WHAT NEITHER PARTY HAS AGREED TO LOSE.

**Cael:** Do you believe Orsol will negotiate?

**Mira:** I believe she will bargain. That is not the same thing.

Mira asked the lens to transmit a proposal to the hidden city: an emergency delegation of residents, selected without the Council's approval, to meet in the threshold before the closing.

She included the disputed clause from the contract if she had recovered it; otherwise she sent the list of names or the witness's silver clapper as proof that the outer world had begun listening. The glass trembled.

A reply came in writing so pale that Cael had to hold a lamp beneath it.

The people of AsterVale were willing to send representatives. They required that Orsol remove the restrictions on speaking of the missing cities and that the outer islands recognize their right to refuse the agreement. The Chancellor would not like the terms.

Mira liked them rather more than she expected.

She sent the invitation to Orsol without asking permission. The political act was small: a letter written in a room, a name placed beside another name.

It was also the first decision about the hidden cities made by someone other than the people who had hidden them. Mira felt the irony and hoped the delegates would forgive her for it.

## Crossing The Seam

The eastern lens did not open like a door. It became a distance.

One moment Mira stood on the marble floor of the observatory with the storm against the windows, and the next the air between her and the market had lengthened into a corridor of pale blue weather. The corridor had no walls.

Along its sides hung fragments of places that had been taken from the world: a bridge ending above a river with no name, a bakery window where bread cooled without an oven, a child's shoes beside a staircase that went upward forever.

The objects did not move when she approached. They seemed to be holding still to avoid being noticed.

Cael tested the first step with the caution of a sailor placing his foot on a gangplank that had broken under him before. His scar shone beneath his cuff. Each step drew an echo from a different direction.

Mira heard her father's voice explaining how to read the tides, then a woman's laugh she had not thought about for years, then the sound of a tram bell in Bellwether. She knew the last sound was real.

She could no longer decide whether the other two were memories or something the lens had made from her longing.

**Mira:** If it shows us what we want, we must not follow it blindly.

**Cael:** It isn't showing me what I want.

He was looking at a small room suspended above the corridor, lit by a single oil lamp. A coat hung beside the door. On the table lay two cups, one with a chipped rim. The room was so ordinary that Mira almost missed it.

Cael had stopped breathing. He said a person had once waited for him in that room and he had promised to return before the lamp went out. He could not recall the person's face.

He could remember the particular shame of making a promise he had not been able to keep.

There was no safe way to pass the room. The corridor curved toward it, following some rule of memory rather than geometry. Mira wanted to hurry him onward.

Then she remembered the dockmaster darkening names on an arrival register and the child who had wound a mechanical bird three times before its message returned. Perhaps the corridor was asking them to look at what they had tried to ignore.

Or perhaps the corridor was merely cruel. The machine had never been required to distinguish between those possibilities.

They entered the room. It was empty except for the cups and the lamp. On the mantel lay a collection of postcards showing places Mira had never visited. A seaside town was painted in blue.

A narrow valley held a bridge that seemed too delicate to bear a cart. A lighthouse stood on a cliff above water the color of iron. The messages on the backs were written in several hands, all addressed to a person named simply HOME.

Someone had collected the cards not to preserve geography but to preserve the hope that the sender might be understood.

Cael took the chipped cup. It was warm. He said he remembered a morning on a ship when he had burned his hand carrying tea through a storm.

A person with dark hair had laughed at him and taken the cup away before he could drop it. He did not know who the person was, but he remembered that laughter had made the storm less frightening.

The room offered him no answer beyond the fragment. Mira let the silence remain.

Mira stayed by the door while Cael examined the room. She knew he might read her distance as suspicion. She also knew that trust could not be manufactured out of urgency. When he returned, she asked whether he wanted to speak.

He shook his head. They continued with a little more space between them, but he did not leave her behind.

Outside, the corridor was changing. The fragments of lost places had begun to turn toward AsterVale like flowers toward a sun. The city's lamps shone at the far end, visible through a veil of rain.

For the first time Mira noticed footprints on the pale floor. Some were enormous; some belonged to children.

The prints came from the direction of the market and stopped here, as though a thousand people had tried to leave and encountered a limit they could not name.

Mira crouched to examine the nearest print. Its outline was smudged by the marks of a hand. Someone had knelt beside it, trying to find a way to lift the person who had been stopped.

A button lay in the center, blue and worn almost smooth. She recognized the type from coats sold in the city's northern quarter before the Council closed the passage. She put it in her pocket beside the brass whistle.

Evidence was accumulating around her in the shapes of things people had touched.

The machine spoke without words. The air thickened, and suddenly she knew the price the old agreement demanded. To cross the passage, a traveler must surrender an exact memory of the person whose return they most desired. The memory was not destroyed immediately.

It became part of the energy that held the boundary closed. Travelers had been paying the cost for generations without knowing there was a toll. The city could welcome them; the world would gradually make them unable to find it again.

**Cael:** Is that what happened to me?

**Mira:** It may be part of it. The passage takes the thing most likely to bring you back.

Cael looked toward the lamps. His expression did not change, but he took the old watch from his coat and placed it in Mira's hand.

He asked her to remember that it had belonged to him, even if he forgot the reason he had carried it. He did not ask her to tell him what he would be losing.

There were some negotiations a person could not conduct without knowing enough to refuse.

Mira turned the watch over. On the back, beneath the scratches of years, was a tiny engraving of a bird in flight. It was the same shape as the brass whistle in her pocket. Both might have come from the same workshop.

She wondered how many people had carried pieces of the same vanished world without recognizing them.

**Mira:** We won't pay the toll.

**Cael:** Then how do we cross?

**Mira:** We make the passage remember that we have refused.

She opened the atlas and wrote the sentence in her own ledger before speaking it aloud. The letters shone against the blue floor. A second set appeared beside them in Cael's hand, though he had not held a pen. The corridor trembled.

For a moment every fragment of place seemed to lean toward the words, and Mira saw how thoroughly the old agreement had depended on travelers accepting costs they did not know they were paying.

The lens did not shatter. It did something harder. It hesitated. The distance to the city shortened by one small step. Mira took that step without surrendering the watch. Cael followed.

Above them, the first thunder of the storm sounded like a door being closed somewhere far away. The corridor lengthened again, then shortened, then began to dissolve into air that smelled of oranges and coal smoke.

They emerged under an arch whose stones were covered with the names of people who had tried to return.

The ledger from the eastern wing grew warm beneath Mira's arm. Its pages opened to the names of the first families selected for the experiments. The arch seemed to recognize the record as an accusation.

Several of the oldest carved names brightened, and Mira understood that proof could become a key when someone had tried to make ignorance a lock.

Before them lay AsterVale. It was neither paradise nor prison in the theatrical sense Mira had expected. A delivery cart had overturned in the street, and two children were trying to help its driver retrieve a scattered load of onions.

A woman scolded them for getting under the wheels. The driver thanked them anyway. A city that could still be irritated about onions, Mira thought, had not entirely surrendered to the terms of its captivity.

## The Train With No Destination

The tramline beyond the boundary had been laid long before AsterVale disappeared from the official maps.

Its rails were made of dark blue metal that did not rust, and its stations were marked by bells whose notes were tuned to different parts of the city.

Travelers once identified their stops by sound as well as by name, a convenience for those who could not read. Now the bells rang at unpredictable intervals and the route maps showed stations that had been removed from the world.

A tram waited at the nearest platform with its doors open and no driver at the controls.

Mira examined the timetable. It listed departures at every hour of the day, but the destinations were blank. Someone had pasted a note beneath the schedule: IF YOU KNOW WHERE YOU ARE GOING, PLEASE TELL THE CONDUCTOR.

She laughed, then found she could not laugh for long. A child stood at the far end of the platform holding a bouquet of flowers. The flowers were fresh.

The child looked at the empty space beyond the rails as though waiting for someone to arrive from a place nobody would name.

Cael approached the tram. Its windows were fogged on the inside, and beneath the fog he could see the outlines of passengers.

They were sitting in ordinary positions: a man reading a newspaper, a woman carrying a basket, two children leaning against each other in sleep. When Cael touched the doorframe, the figures vanished. The seats remained warm.

The tram had been carrying the memory of a journey even after the people who made it were no longer present.

**Mira:** We should walk. This could be part of the machine's accounting system.

**Cael:** Or it could be the only way people here have to reach the market.

The conductor appeared at the rear door. He was old, with a uniform that had been repaired so many times it was more patches than cloth. He asked for tickets.

Mira showed him the railway ticket from Bellwether if she carried it, or the note from her ledger if she did not. He studied the paper and nodded. It did not matter what she had shown him.

He said he accepted any evidence that a traveler had somewhere she wished to go.

They boarded. The conductor closed the doors and pulled a lever. The tram moved without a sound. Outside the windows, the city passed through several versions of itself. In one, the streets were crowded with festival banners.

In another, rain fell on houses whose doors had been boarded shut. In a third, a line of people waited outside a hospital, holding blankets over their heads while the sky glowed blue with a light that did not belong to the sun.

The conductor said the tram had always taken passengers through the city's memories. The Council had forbidden it because the route made it difficult to maintain a single approved account of the catastrophe.

Cael watched the platforms pass. He recognized one from childhood: a narrow stop beside a clock that ran backward. He had stood there with his sister waiting for a train that would take them to the upper docks.

She had been angry because he would not let her carry the ticket. He had told her the ticket was important, and she had replied that she was more important than the ticket. He remembered the argument now with a painful clarity.

He had never imagined the memory would survive while her face disappeared.

**Cael:** I should have given her the ticket.

**Mira:** You were a child.

**Cael:** So was she. That didn't make her less right.

The tram passed a schoolhouse where a woman stood at the door with a bell in her hand. The pupils were filing inside, each carrying a book bound in a different color. Mira saw her mother's face among the adults.

The image lasted only an instant. She rose from her seat so quickly that the conductor put out his hand to steady her. The schoolhouse was gone. Beyond the next window lay a canal lined with lanterns.

Mira sat down again and pressed her fingers against the glass.

**Mira:** I saw her.

**Cael:** Your mother?

**Mira:** She was standing at the door. I don't know whether it was a memory or something the tram wanted me to see.

The conductor told them that the route did not manufacture memories. It carried the impressions left by people who had traveled through the city, preserved in the metal of the rails and the wood of the carriages.

The impressions could be incomplete or misleading, but they were not invented. He had spent forty years trying to explain this to officials who wanted a timetable that could be filed under one heading. He had finally stopped explaining and continued driving.

At the next stop, a woman carrying a basket of pears boarded. She looked at Mira with a brief expression of recognition, then took a seat beside the window. Her coat was red.

The star embroidered on its collar had been repaired with blue thread. Mira wanted to ask whether she knew the child whose name had disappeared from the coast. Before she could speak, the woman opened her basket and offered a pear to the conductor.

He accepted it, thanked her by a name Mira did not hear, and continued down the aisle.

Mira followed the woman with her eyes. The seat beside her was empty, but an indentation in the cushion suggested that someone had been sitting there moments before. Mira could feel the question rising in her throat. She stopped it.

The woman had come to ride the tram, not to become evidence in a stranger's investigation. Mira waited until the next stop, then asked whether the embroidered star had been made by someone she loved. The woman touched it and smiled sadly.

She said yes. She did not say more. Mira thanked her and returned to her seat.

The tram crossed a bridge suspended above a city that could be seen only from one direction. The towers below were built of pale stone and copper roofs, and the streets glowed with warm light. Cael leaned forward.

He recognized the market where he had once bought pastries with Elian. The memory came without warning and without the ache of being taken away.

For a moment he was simply a brother remembering a day on which his sister had laughed at the crumbs on his coat.

The conductor rang the bell. He announced the next stop as ASTERVALE MARKET, VIA THE LONG WAY HOME. The passengers began gathering their belongings. Some had not been visible when the journey began. Others had become clearer with each station.

A little boy ran down the aisle carrying a paper airplane. The conductor caught it before it struck the window, folded its wings more carefully, and returned it. The boy thanked him.

The ordinary kindness made Mira more hopeful than any vision the tram had shown.

They stepped onto the platform. The conductor handed Mira a ticket stamped with the date of their arrival. It was not a magical object. It was a piece of paper bearing the name of a city.

Mira put it in her ledger beside the other evidence. The tram moved away, its windows reflecting the market lamps. She could not tell whether the passengers inside were real or remembered.

She knew the conductor had stopped when they needed him to stop, and that the ticket gave her a place to begin asking questions.

## The Ferry Of Blue Glass

The train had carried them to the edge of a city that had not appeared on any chart Mira trusted. Beyond the last platform the street ended at a canal, and the canal ended in an expanse of blue glass.

It looked like water only when the wind moved across it; otherwise it reflected not the sky above but a sky that belonged to a different hour.

The glass was crowded with faint luminous shapes, as if hundreds of lanterns had been lowered beneath its surface and left there to wait for night.

A ferry stood at the quay with its gangplank down.

Its cabin was painted green, its ropes wound into precise circles on the deck, and its brass bell had been polished so thoroughly that Mira could see a distorted image of her own face in it. There was no crew.

The notice nailed to the ticket window stated that passage would be granted to travelers with a destination, and that travelers without a destination would be returned to the shore they most wished to forget.

**Cael:** That seems a poor way to run a public ferry.

**Mira:** It may have been designed by the same people who believed a public road should be kept safe by removing everyone who tried to use it.

A boy at the end of the quay was trying to drag a wheeled cart toward the gangplank. The cart was piled with jars of preserved pears. Each jar held a small paper label bearing a name and a date.

The boy's left foot was in a splint, and he had been pulling the cart with one hand while the other gripped a piece of rope.

Every time he reached the edge of the plank the wheels rolled backward, as if the ferry had rejected him.

His name was Daro. He said the pears had come from his aunt's orchard beyond the glass.

The orchard had been sold to pay a debt the city insisted his aunt had never incurred, and Daro was carrying the final harvest to relatives on the other shore. He had the addresses written on a postcard.

When Mira asked to see it, he unfolded a rectangle of blue paper on which the ink moved whenever the light changed.

Daro: I had six addresses this morning. Now there are three. If the ferry doesn't take me soon there won't be any left, and my cousins will think I promised to visit and never came.

The ferryman appeared then, though neither Mira nor Cael had seen him approach. He was a tall man in a coat stitched from pieces of outdated maps.

His left sleeve displayed a coastline that had been revised three times; his right carried the outline of an island whose name had been scratched away.

He introduced himself as Fen and said that he would be happy to accept their fares once the young man finished explaining where he intended to go.

**Mira:** He has an address. It's disappearing. That is why he needs the ferry.

Fen: The crossing is maintained by the distinction between one place and another. A destination that cannot be named is a hole in the contract. If I drive my vessel into it, I may carry all of you somewhere that cannot bring you home.

**Cael:** What did you carry before the contract?

Fen looked down at the rope in his hands. He said he had carried people. He spoke as if this were an obsolete profession, like a maker of clock hands or an apprentice assigned to copy entire books by candlelight.

He had spent his first twenty years crossing the canal before the eastern lens was built. Back then the banks had been ordinary stone and the water brown from the mills upstream. The ferry had no bell because a passenger could shout for it.

Now the bell answered questions the ferryman no longer felt permitted to understand.

Mira studied the glass. Under its surface the shapes were not lanterns. They were fragments of written journeys: a child visiting her grandmother, a machinist crossing for a new job, a couple returning from a wedding, a woman traveling without telling anyone why.

Every journey had been converted into an entry with a departure and arrival. The spaces between entries were filled with tiny, motionless stars.

Somewhere in the structure of the canal the machine had tried to turn the act of going somewhere into a form of permission.

Daro began to cry without sound. He rubbed his eyes with the sleeve of his jacket and then became angry at himself for crying. Mira knew the anger. She had once been told that a scientist should never allow grief to interfere with measurement.

She had spent the next decade discovering that grief could be measured very precisely and still could not be cured by the accuracy of the measurement.

She asked Fen whether he could take them across if they provided a temporary address. He refused.

A temporary address, he said, might become permanent, and then the machine would have to decide whether it was telling the truth when it held the shore in place. Cael offered to repair the ferry's cracked handrail while they waited.

Fen said the rail had been cracked for forty years and was not a priority. Cael took a wrench from his coat and began tightening the fittings anyway.

The repair changed something. A small sound traveled along the deck, not the ring of the brass bell but the soft click of iron settling against wood. The ferry shuddered. One of the stars beneath the glass moved.

Mira looked more closely at the inscription on the ticket window. It was a legal instruction from the Council, but there was a second set of marks below it, nearly worn away: CARRIED PERSONS HAVE THE RIGHT TO DISEMBARK.

**Mira:** The old rules are still here. They have been overwritten, not erased.

Fen: That language is older than the agreement. It describes a ferry. We are no longer a ferry.

**Cael:** You are standing on one. I'm mending the rail. There's a boy with a cart, and the water is below us. Perhaps the paperwork has become confused about what you are.

Fen's expression changed. He had not smiled since his arrival, but now he seemed in danger of remembering how. He moved to the rail and helped Cael hold the bracket steady while the last screw was turned. Daro pushed his cart onto the gangplank.

The wheels caught at the edge and stopped. Mira crouched, slid a plank under them, and pushed. The cart rolled aboard with a clatter that echoed across the glass.

The bell rang once. Beyond the quay a line appeared in the water, bright and blue, stretching toward the other shore. Fen climbed into the cabin and took the wheel. He did not ask for fares. He asked whether everyone could swim.

When no one answered immediately, he handed out life belts from a locker that smelled of salt and old oranges. Mira tied hers with more care than dignity. Cael checked Daro's splint before fastening the boy's belt.

They moved out onto the glass. At first the motion was familiar: a little rise beneath the keel, the soft slap of water against wood. Then the canal began showing them other journeys.

Mira saw a younger version of herself walking beside her father through a crowded street. Cael saw the back of a woman wearing a blue scarf.

Daro saw his aunt at an orchard gate, holding a basket of pears and smiling as if she had never sold the land. The images floated just beyond reach. The ferry passed through them without breaking their surfaces.

**Mira:** Don't try to touch the water.

**Cael:** I wasn't going to.

**Mira:** You were leaning over the rail.

**Cael:** I was wondering whether the people we see are memories or people who still exist somewhere.

**Mira:** We may have to let that remain a question until we know how to ask them.

Halfway across, the ferry stopped. The blue line beneath it broke into three parallel tracks, each leading toward a different version of the opposite shore. On one stood a crowd waiting at a market gate. On another stood an empty field.

On the third the buildings were familiar, but their shadows fell upward into the clouds and a river ran through the sky. Fen gripped the wheel and whispered that the contract had never presented him with three destinations before.

A voice rose from below the deck. It was the ferry's voice, high and formal: THE NAMED DESTINATION IS NO LONGER AVAILABLE. ACCEPT A SUBSTITUTION. The words repeated until Fen covered his ears. Daro clung to his cart.

One of the jars fell and rolled toward the edge. Cael caught it with his boot.

Mira took the postcard from Daro's hand. Only one address remained. She wrote it on the back of her father's letter, then copied it again in the atlas. The letters held steady. The machine repeated its request, louder.

Mira looked at the three tracks and understood that they were not offering destinations. They were offering revisions to Daro's intention.

The crowd would make his journey a public delivery, the field would make it a journey that had never happened, and the upside-down city would make it a story the machine could classify as impossible.

**Mira:** We are not choosing a replacement. We are going where he asked to go.

The voice replied that the requested place could not be verified. Mira asked Fen how he had navigated when there were no contracts. He said he had followed the current and the lights along the shore. She asked whether he could still see them.

He closed his eyes, then opened them again and looked past the three false destinations. At the far edge of the glass a single ordinary window shone in a dark building. Someone had hung a lamp outside it. The light did not flicker.

It was the kind of light a person kept burning because they were expecting someone home.

Fen: I know that lamp. I used to steer by it when the current was bad. The woman in that house made terrible tea and never let anyone leave without a second cup.

**Cael:** Then we should arrive before she gets worried about the first.

Fen turned the wheel toward the lamp. The three tracks collapsed into one, and the ferry lurched forward with such force that every jar in Daro's cart rattled. The machine shouted something about unauthorized passage. Its voice grew thin as the wind rose.

For a moment the water below them became ordinary again, cold and dark and full of small ripples where the moonlight struck it.

On the far shore, Daro's cousins were waiting. They had been given the wrong arrival date and had come on the chance that some part of the letter was still true. Daro shouted when he recognized them.

He did not run because of his splint. He stood on the deck and waved both arms until they reached him, and then he cried without trying to hide it.

Fen helped carry the cart onto the quay. Before he returned to his vessel, he removed the notice from the ticket window. He folded it carefully and placed it in the cabin beneath the logbook of his earlier crossings.

He said he did not yet know which authority would allow him to keep operating if he refused to demand a destination that could be verified.

Cael replied that the people waiting on both shores might turn out to be an authority worth listening to.

Mira recorded the crossing in the atlas. She did not write that they had defeated the machine, because the machine was still operating and had done enough harm to make a single victory feel dishonest.

She wrote that a ferryman had remembered a light, a courier had repaired a railing, a child had protected a harvest, and a boy had reached his family without surrendering the purpose of his journey to an instrument.

Daro gave them one jar of pears. He said he did not have money to pay for the crossing, and his aunt had always said that a debt paid in food was more likely to be remembered kindly. Mira accepted the jar.

Cael opened it with the edge of his pocketknife as they climbed the street toward the market. They ate the pears with their fingers, laughing at how difficult it was to divide an uneven number of slices equally.

The city around them was still hidden, still frightened, still caught inside a bargain that had stolen the shape of tomorrow.

But the pears tasted like an ordinary afternoon, and for the length of that walk Mira allowed herself to believe ordinary afternoons might return.

## The Quay Of Unreturned Things

The ferry delivered them to a quay that could not have appeared in any official harbor chart. Its pilings were made from old street lamps, the lanterns submerged in blue water below the boarding deck.

Little fish moved through the glass chimneys, brushing against soot that had been deposited in some other lifetime. Across the channel, steps rose from the water into a town of balconies and sloping roofs. Every building bore a small white tile above its door.

Some tiles held a name. Others held a question mark written in the same careful blue glaze.

Cael stepped off the ferry with the ingrained caution of someone who had learned that a dock might not welcome the weight of a messenger. He tested a plank, moved the heavy case of petitions onto solid ground, and offered Mira a hand.

She accepted it, then noticed that he had been watching her instead of the quay. When she asked whether he was afraid, he said he was counting the ways they might get back. The ferry's captain had already begun untying the rope.

A return journey was not guaranteed, she had told them, but a ticket could be saved and used if the river remembered its own direction.

There was a market along the quay, but it did not trade in anything a visitor would immediately recognize. A woman sold bundles of keys. Her sign read DOORS YOU MAY HAVE FORGOTTEN.

A boy sat beneath a canopy repairing the straps of suitcases that belonged to travelers who had never arrived. Beside the steps, a man in a yellow apron weighed small parcels on a brass balance.

The parcels contained wedding invitations, school certificates, worn-out gloves, fragments of recipes, and a child's collection of painted stones. A queue had formed before his table.

People spoke to him in hushed voices and carried away the things he gave them as if receiving them made their hands heavier.

Mira approached the table and asked who owned the parcels. The man looked at her atlas, recognized its binding, and put down the small weight he was holding. His name was Parel, he said, and he was the keeper of unreturned things.

The goods on his table had been separated from their owners by the boundary, the machine, or the ordinary carelessness of people in a hurry. He did not decide to whom they belonged.

He asked claimants to describe something about the object that was not visible from its exterior.

Parel: The lock of a suitcase tells us nothing. A person might have bought it yesterday or inherited it from a woman they never met.

But the smell of the clothes inside, the sound the hinge makes when it is opened in winter—that can be a beginning.

**Mira:** What happens if two people describe the same object?

Parel: Then we discover that belonging is not as simple as possession. They may have to speak to one another. Sometimes they would rather leave it here.

The keeper offered them tea in cups that did not match. Cael held his cup with both hands. He had been staring at a parcel wrapped in dark oilcloth ever since they reached the table.

There was no name on it, only a small brass tag stamped VEY. Mira had seen that surname on his courier license, and on none of the family records he had tried to obtain. She kept her gaze on the keeper.

Cael had spent too long being treated as an object of investigation for her to turn the moment into another interview conducted without his permission.

Parel noticed the parcel. He took it from the shelf and put it on the table without pushing it toward Cael. He asked whether the courier wanted to make a claim. Cael said he did not know if he could describe what was inside.

Parel explained that remembering could be a reason to claim something, but it could not be a requirement for receiving help. Sometimes the person most entitled to a thing was the person whose memory had been damaged by its loss.

**Cael:** If I take it and it isn't mine, I've stolen somebody's past.

Parel: If you refuse to look because you might be wrong, you may leave somebody's past here forever. We cannot remove the risk. We can make you answerable for the choice.

Mira asked whether they could open the parcel in the keeper's presence, leaving it in his custody until Cael knew what it was. The keeper considered that a sensible compromise. He unfolded the oilcloth.

Inside lay a pair of child's shoes, a broken miniature compass, and a small envelope addressed in handwriting so familiar to Cael that he stopped breathing. The envelope did not contain a letter.

It contained a pressed sprig of the apple blossom that grew on the lower islands, its petals now brown and fragile. Beneath it was a scrap of paper with a sentence: WHEN YOU COME BACK, TELL ME WHICH WAY THE RAIN WENT.

Cael read the sentence without sound. His face changed. Mira recognized the expression from the bell garden, where a weather memory had arrived without any of the events that should have surrounded it. He knew the rain had fallen sideways.

He knew it had smelled of apples. Now the sentence gave the memory another person's expectation, a promise held in a piece of paper long after the person who made it had vanished from his history.

**Mira:** Do you know who wrote it?

**Cael:** I know I promised to answer. I don't know to whom. That's worse than not remembering at all.

Parel: Then we'll keep the parcel in your name for one week. You can come back with what you learn. Or you can ask for it to be held longer. I don't charge for either choice.

They went to the edge of the quay, where a row of round windows opened beneath the waterline. Through them Mira could see a street suspended inside the channel, its stones clean and dry, its shops lit by yellow lamps.

A woman walked along the submerged pavement carrying a basket of bread. When the woman reached the water's edge she passed through the glass reflection of a staircase and vanished from view.

Mira understood that the quay was built along several different histories at once. People passed through different layers of the city without necessarily meeting. It was less a place than an agreement to share the possibility of arrival.

**Cael:** The market feels ordinary until you look at it long enough.

**Mira:** Perhaps that's what ordinary means here. Being able to do something every day even though the world keeps trying to stop you.

A child appeared beside them, leading a dog that seemed to recognize Mira before the child did. He asked whether she was the cartographer from outside. When she admitted it, he said the quay had been expecting her. Not everyone wanted her to come.

Some people feared that a person carrying an atlas would decide where their streets belonged and send official messengers to move them. Others had kept empty rooms for the people who lived outside the boundary.

The child himself had never seen the world beyond the channel. He said he did not understand why the adults argued about returning to a place he had never visited.

Mira asked what he liked about the quay. He told her the fish changed color when it rained. He liked how the kitchen windows opened over the water.

He liked that the woman who repaired keys would keep fixing a broken one even if a customer had no money. When he was older, he wanted to work on the boats. She asked whether he wanted to leave.

He said he would like to travel once, but he wanted to come home afterward. The desire seemed simple enough to fit into a sentence, and too complicated to fit into any of the Council's categories.

They found the local notice board at the foot of a clock tower. Its postings were written in several alphabets, some older than the language of the observatory. One announced a music lesson. Another warned people not to enter the abandoned waterworks after sunset.

A third contained the dates of an upcoming assembly and a request that visitors who wished to speak register before the hall reached capacity.

Beneath the notices was a blank sheet of paper with a question written in large letters: WHO WILL RECORD WHAT THE OUTSIDE WORLD REFUSES TO HEAR?

Mira took a piece of charcoal from the box provided for replies. She wrote: WE WILL RECORD IT, BUT YOU MUST BE ABLE TO CORRECT US. She put the charcoal down. Cael added: AND IF YOU ASK US TO STOP READING, WE WILL STOP.

Their two sentences looked like modest contributions amid the furious handwriting already covering the board. Several people gathered to look. A woman objected that stopping an archive could become another form of censorship.

A man asked who would preserve statements made by people who could no longer speak. The argument began in earnest before Mira could pretend that she had resolved anything.

A teacher with a small brass whistle introduced herself as Nel. She had come to collect children from a lesson about the old bridges and found the visitors surrounded by adults asking questions.

Nel told the crowd they would have plenty of opportunities to challenge the cartographer at the assembly. Then she turned to Mira and asked whether she wanted to see the school.

She had no authority to speak for the quay, Nel explained, but she did have authority to invite guests into a classroom, so long as the children were told that the guests were free to leave and so were they.

The classroom was upstairs in a former customs house. Its maps showed the streets in several different arrangements, each labeled with the name of the person who had drawn it. One was made of colored thread.

Another had been painted on the back of a sail. The children had placed paper boats along the waterways, each boat bearing a name and a number indicating how many people might travel in it.

When Mira asked why there was no official map on the wall, Nel answered that the official one was in a drawer where the students could consult it when they needed to know which laws would be enforced that week.

Nel: I teach the children what the city looked like according to the people who lived here. Then I teach them the Council's map.

It's important to understand the authority that may try to govern you, but I don't want them to confuse its map with the territory.

A little girl asked whether the outer world still had stars. Mira told her that it did. The girl looked disappointed.

She had hoped the stars belonged to the boundary, because the sky above the city had been unusually bright for as long as she could remember. A boy asked whether people outside ever got hungry.

Another wanted to know whether the rain was blue there too. They treated Mira's answers as stories rather than proof, asking questions when she contradicted their parents and laughing when she admitted she could not explain the color of the clouds.

After class Nel showed them the school register. Several children had been born inside the boundary but recorded as older than their parents according to the Council's outside calendar. The contradiction had caused disputes over apprenticeships and inheritance.

Nel had developed a second register based on seasons spent together rather than official years.

She asked Mira to help her understand how the calendars might be reconciled without allowing the Council to erase a generation simply because its birthdays did not fit the expected arithmetic.

Mira spent an hour comparing the records. The result was incomplete. It could not tell the teacher precisely when the children had been born.

But it showed that the two calendars drifted at a measurable rate and that the drift changed near the blue-glass channels. Nel could use the discovery to prepare evidence for the assembly.

More importantly, the children could now understand why the numbers on the official forms did not always match what their families remembered. Their confusion had been treated as a personal failing. The discrepancy belonged to the machinery, not to them.

When they returned to the quay, the keeper of unreturned things had closed his table for the evening. He was sitting at the edge of the water, eating bread from a paper bag.

He handed Cael a small brass receipt confirming that the parcel would remain in his care. The receipt did not describe the objects inside.

It recorded only the terms they had agreed upon, so a future custodian would know the parcel had not been abandoned.

Parel: A receipt is a small promise. It says a person came here with a question and another person agreed not to make the question disappear.

Cael slipped the paper into his coat. Mira looked back at the quay as its lamps began to brighten. Behind the windows below the water, the submerged street filled with people carrying groceries and children running home from lessons.

The outer pier was crowded with visitors arriving for the assembly, each with a story that would not fit easily into the official accounts. Somewhere in that crowd might be the woman who had asked Cael which way the rain went.

He did not try to find her yet. For the first time, he had something resembling a right to come back.

They followed Nel toward the upper market. Above the steps, an enormous clock showed two different hours on its two faces. The hands were moving at slightly different speeds.

Mira thought about how often she had used the word error to describe a discrepancy between a measurement and an expectation. This time the discrepancy was the evidence.

She opened the atlas to a blank page and drew the quay without trying to decide which of its histories had the better claim to being real.

## Threshold Market

The hidden city began where the eastern lens ended. Its central market occupied a wide circle beneath towers built from gold limestone, each connected to its neighbor by a bridge of dark blue metal. Lanterns hung from cables above the stalls.

Every lantern contained the name of a person who had left the city, as if the people had tried to send their absent loved ones a light without knowing whether a light could cross the boundary.

Mira entered the market by following the smell of oranges. It surprised her that the smell was ordinary. She had expected the air of a hidden city to feel enchanted or poisoned or at least different in a way a careful observer could describe.

Instead the fruit was a little bruised, the stalls were badly balanced, and a man with a remarkable mustache had arranged his oranges in a pyramid that was already leaning toward collapse. One fell.

A girl caught it, tossed it back, and received an insult from the vendor that made them both laugh. Life had not become more symbolic simply because someone had made it secret.

A printer's shop stood under the southern bridge. Its owner, a woman named Ilya, was setting type for a newspaper that could not leave the city. Every day she printed a page headed WHAT WE STILL KNOW.

Under the heading were corrections, births, deaths, the locations of newly opened clinics, and a column for memories people were afraid of losing. The column had once held notices about lost umbrellas and broken engagements. Now it took up most of the page.

A parent described the smell of a baby's hair; an apprentice recorded the sound his master's cough made when a joke was particularly bad; a musician wrote that the third note of her mother's favorite tune should be played a fraction too early.

Ilya told Mira that she had been accused of sentimentalism by the city government. She pointed to the wall of proofs behind her, where the editions hung one over another like an uneven tapestry.

Ilya's last issue had printed a map of streets that no longer led anywhere. A month before that, she had published the names of three men who worked at the boundary and were lost during a night shift.

The official report described the incident as a disruption of access. The newspaper described their families. The two accounts were not contradictory, Ilya said. One was simply smaller than the truth.

**Mira:** Do the people read it?

Ilya: Some read it. Some burn it to keep warm. I think both are reasons to print another copy.

Cael noticed a sheet hanging near the press and stopped. It showed an engraving of a ship approaching AsterVale from the upper cloudline. The lettering beneath had been cut away, but he recognized the rigging. He had sailed on the ship.

In the engraving someone stood at the stern, one hand raised in farewell. He could not see her face, but he knew how that person leaned when the wind came from the east. For a moment his composure fell from him entirely.

Mira did not ask for an explanation. She took the image down and paid for it.

They carried the engraving through streets bright with laundry, past a school where children were drawing maps of their bedrooms in order to learn what the word geography had once included. In one map the bed occupied the whole page.

In another the doorway led directly to the moon. The teacher had written beneath each drawing: A PLACE IS NOT ONLY WHERE IT IS. It was a simple sentence.

Mira wanted to return to the observatory and place it beside every theorem her father had ever written.

Under the western bridge they found a shop repairing mechanical birds. Each bird contained a tiny folded paper on which a patron could write a name. When wound, it would fly a short, uncertain circle and return to the hand that released it.

Mira watched a boy set one loose over the canal. It landed on the wrong balcony. The boy laughed, tried again, and on the third attempt the bird returned to him. The message inside read: COME HOME WHEN YOU CAN. There was no address.

There never had been.

The city, she thought, had kept inventing ways to send love across an absence. These inventions would be called inefficient by a machine designed to preserve exact quantities.

They were, to Mira, the strongest evidence that the machine had never understood what it was preserving.

Vendors sold the ordinary necessities of life: tea, flour, shoelaces, maps of streets that ended at the city's invisible border. The people had adapted to their confinement with the inventiveness that makes it possible to live inside almost any injustice.

Children had made a game of throwing stones against the glass wall and watching them return, older than they had been when they left a hand.

The bakers traded recipes from memory because none of their old books could be read by anyone outside the boundary.

Mira stood near a fountain whose water flowed upward and read a notice on the town board. It announced a missing person's meeting, a performance by the winter choir, and the selection of delegates to the Emergency Assembly.

The last announcement was written in nervous handwriting, as though the town clerk had not expected the word emergency to become an everyday part of civic life.

Cael was searching every face. Mira did not need to ask whom he hoped to find. He had not spoken Elian's name since Orsol accused him of being unfit to remember her.

He touched the leather strap of his satchel whenever someone with a blue scarf passed.

**Mira:** There are thousands of people here. We will have to search with more than our eyes.

**Cael:** I remember where she used to sit when we bought pastries. If there is a street with a clock that runs backward, the shop may still be there.

The clock ran backward. A small boy was winding it from a ladder, laughing because he had been told the clock had always done that and saw no reason it should change.

A woman behind a counter recognized Cael's scar before she recognized his face. She put down a tray of sugared pears and began to cry with the calm certainty of someone who had waited too long to be surprised.

Baker: You're one of the messengers. We were told you could come back only if the cities agreed to forget you.

**Cael:** I didn't agree to forget anyone.

Baker: It is possible that the people who signed for you did not ask.

She told them that Elian had been taken to the House of Returns, a school for children whose families were outside the boundary.

The children were taught the names of cities they could not reach, but not the faces of the people who had loved them. The Chancellor had insisted it would be kinder not to encourage impossible hopes.

Mira looked at the row of lamps above the counter, each carrying a name in blue glass. One lamp had gone dark. Beside it, someone had set an empty saucer for a person who was expected to come home.

They walked toward the House of Returns through streets that changed names at every turn. Mira began to understand why the City of AsterVale had built such elaborate signs.

If you could not leave a place, you made the place contain enough language that it could continue to feel like a world.

At a corner near the canal they passed a procession of children carrying paper birds. A girl looked directly at Cael. She wore a blue scarf and had a small white scar above one eyebrow. He stopped in the middle of the street.

**Cael:** Elian?

The girl looked at him as though at a picture from a book whose title she could not remember. Then she reached into her pocket and brought out a window she had drawn on a folded piece of paper.

She had made it as a child to help her belongings feel less lonely. Mira recognized the detail from Cael's account. The girl was older than the one he remembered, perhaps sixteen. Time had continued for her even when memory had not.

Elian: I used to dream there was someone standing outside my room. He always had a red kite and told me it was really a bird because he couldn't make the wings stay straight.

**Cael:** It was a terrible kite.

Elian: You know it?

**Cael:** I made it.

She looked down at the folded paper. Her mouth trembled as she tried to fit a face to the shape of a memory. For a moment she seemed to be deciding whether to run away from the possibility or toward it.

Then she stepped closer and asked him a question no one else in the city could have answered.

Elian: When we were frightened of the thunder, what did we call it?

**Cael:** A giant moving the furniture upstairs.

She laughed. It was the first sound Mira had heard from her that was not an effort to remember something. Elian held out her hand, and Cael took it. The scar on his wrist glowed faintly.

The lamps above the market were still burning, but one had begun to shine brighter.

Mira turned away, not from embarrassment, but to give the reunion an ordinary space in which to happen. She was glad she had not promised him that his sister would return. She had promised only that they would find out.

The difference was finally a kindness instead of a failure.

From every bell tower came a deep metallic tone. A printed notice appeared on the market board: THE AGREEMENT WILL CLOSE AT FIRST LIGHT. ALL RESIDENTS MUST REMAIN IN THEIR DESIGNATED DISTRICTS.

The Chancellor's authority had found a way to cross the boundary without asking the people who lived behind it.

AsterVale's mayor stood before the fountain and read the notice aloud. The crowd listened in silence until a woman near the front asked whether the agreement required their consent.

The mayor admitted that no copy of the original contract had ever been shown to the city. A man selling vegetables demanded to know why a contract he had never seen could govern his grandchildren.

The murmuring grew into voices, then arguments, then the beginning of a public meeting.

Mira had spent years believing arguments were things people made when they had forgotten the value of evidence.

Watching the market, she began to suspect that an argument could also be a kind of evidence: that a person cared enough about the future to speak where a command would have preferred silence.

## Chamber Heard

The Council chamber was circular and had no chair at its center. The architect had explained that no one should govern a country from a position where every other person had to look up.

The Chancellor had subsequently arranged for her own seat to be built on a platform at the edge, where everyone still had to look up at her from almost every angle.

In the locked desk Mira found the amendments to the agreement. The original instrument had not been designed to erase cities. It had been designed to preserve names long enough for strangers to find one another across a storm.

The Council had modified it when the first rescue required more power than the ordinary skies could provide. Every payment of a remembered person had increased the instrument's capacity to keep the cities separate.

The final amendment gave the Chancellor authority to choose the next payment without consulting the people affected. A list of proposed payments included the city of Bellwether and, beneath it, the name of the observatory itself.

**Mira:** The machine was built to remember. We changed it into an instrument for forgetting.

She took the amendments to the assembly. She would not spare the Council the humiliation of having its words read aloud in front of the people who had paid for them.

## The Couriers Oath

Elian had asked Cael to meet her after the family hearings in a narrow courtyard behind the bakery. She had chosen the place because it was where they used to sit as children when the summer heat made their home unbearable.

The courtyard was smaller than Cael remembered. A fig tree filled most of it, and its roots had lifted the paving stones into uneven steps. On the wall someone had painted the outline of a ship with blue sails.

Cael recognized the ship but not the hand that had painted it.

Elian arrived carrying a small wooden box. She set it between them and opened the lid. Inside were objects that had survived the erasures because their owners had hidden them in places the Council's clerks did not know to search: a tin whistle, a

pair of mismatched gloves, a sailor's knot tied in red cord, and a photograph of two children standing beside a broken clock. Cael knew the boy was himself. The girl was Elian.

The knowledge arrived before the feeling, as though he were reading a caption beneath a stranger's portrait.

Elian: You promised to teach me to tie that knot.

**Cael:** I did teach you. I remember the knot. I don't remember the lesson.

Elian: You made me do it twenty times until I could do it with my eyes closed. Then you told me not to tell anyone because you'd have to invent a harder knot for me to learn next.

He laughed. The story sounded like something he might have said. He could not remember saying it. Elian seemed to notice the difference between recognition and memory. She did not demand that he pretend otherwise.

She placed the red cord in his hand and waited while he tried to tie the knot.

The first attempt failed. His fingers knew the movements, but the cord slipped. He tried again, slowly, letting the motion carry him through a sequence his mind could not name. On the third attempt the knot held. Elian's face changed.

For a moment she was the younger sister who had waited by the dock for his return, then the adult woman who had lived through years without him, then both at once. She took the knot and pulled it tight.

Elian: I wanted you to come back because I was angry. I thought if you came back I could tell you exactly what it had been like to wait. Now you're here, and I don't know where to begin.

**Cael:** You can begin anywhere. I don't deserve a special order.

She shook her head. She said she had spent years hearing people explain why the absence had been necessary, why the city must be kept hidden, why the people who left could not return.

Every explanation had made the loss sound like an administrative result. She had wanted to be angry at a person.

Cael's return had given her a person to look at, and the anger had become tangled with relief in a way she did not know how to describe.

Mira stood at the courtyard gate and listened to the sounds from the market. She had offered to leave them alone, but Elian had asked her to stay. The request made Mira uneasy. She had known Cael only a short time.

She was not his family, and she did not wish to become a witness who claimed authority over a history she had not lived.

Yet Elian seemed to want a third person present to remember the conversation afterward, in case the machine took another piece of it from one of them.

Elian took out the photograph. On the back someone had written a date and a place name that neither sibling recognized. Below it was a sentence in the handwriting of their father: WHEN THE SKY BREAKS, FIND THE ONE WHO KNOWS THE WAY HOME.

Cael read it twice. He remembered their father as a man who worked the upper docks and repaired ships, but he could not remember why the sentence would have been written on a family photograph.

**Cael:** I thought he died in the northern storm.

Elian: That's what the Council told us. The truth is that he disappeared after refusing to sign the first agreement. I found the records years later. They called it a voluntary absence.

Cael lowered the photograph. For years he had carried guilt for failing to return to a sister whose name he could not remember. Now he learned that their father had been taken by the same system. The knowledge did not remove his guilt.

It changed the shape of it. He had not been the only person who failed to come home. The boundary had made home into a place that could be denied to people who most needed it.

Mira examined the handwriting and recognized the same blue ink used in the early Council petitions. She could not prove that the siblings' father had worked with her own father, but the dates overlapped.

She told them what she knew and what she did not. Elian asked whether the records could be made public. Mira said yes, if the witnesses consented and the documents were preserved without editing out the disagreements.

Elian looked relieved that the answer contained a condition she could understand.

Elian asked whether Cael still carried the watch their mother had given him. He took it from his coat. Its face was blank, its hands removed. She told him that the watch had never kept time properly.

Their mother had given it to him because he was always late and said a broken watch would at least be honest about the problem. Cael remembered the joke then, not as words but as a warm sound in a kitchen.

His eyes filled with tears. Elian looked away to give him a moment in which the feeling could belong to him.

They spent the rest of the afternoon telling small stories. Elian described the winter when the canal froze and children skated beneath the bridges. Cael remembered the smell of the bakery after rain.

Mira told them about the first time she saw Bellwether's harbor, standing beside her father on a tram platform. The stories did not fit into a single history. Some contradicted one another. Elian insisted that the clock in the courtyard had always run backward.

Cael said it had broken only after the boundary closed. Mira found a newspaper clipping suggesting both were partly right. They laughed and kept talking.

As evening approached, Elian returned the objects to the box. She gave Cael the red cord and the photograph. He asked whether she was certain. She said she had kept them for years because she hoped he would need them. Now he did.

She kept the tin whistle, though it no longer made a sound. She said their mother had once tried to teach her to play it and that she intended to finish the lesson when she could find someone who remembered the tune.

Elian: I don't want you to promise never to leave again. I know you have a life outside these walls.

**Cael:** I can promise to tell you when I go, and where I think I'm going.

Elian: And if you forget the way back?

**Cael:** Then I hope you'll be allowed to come looking for me.

They embraced. It was not the reunion Cael had imagined when he first began to remember her name. It contained anger, confusion, affection, and the knowledge that neither could recover the years taken from them.

Mira watched from the gate and understood that returning a person to the world did not return the time they had lost. The work of being family would have to begin again, in the present, where both siblings had changed.

When they left the courtyard, the market lamps were being lit. Elian walked with them as far as the assembly square, then turned back to help the baker carry bread to the meeting. Cael watched her go.

He tied the red cord around his wrist above the compass scar. Mira thought of the old courier's promise: to deliver what had been entrusted to him, to return when he could, and to tell the truth when he could not.

The promise had been broken by the machine. It could be made again by the people who survived it.

## Night Before Vote

By evening, the market had become a different place. Traders dismantled their stalls and carried the wooden frames to the assembly square to make benches.

The candles that had been set out for a festival were moved under awnings to shelter the ballot tables from rain. The city had expected to celebrate the turn of the season.

Instead it was preparing to decide whether the season itself might be permitted to change.

Mira and Cael walked beside the canal with Ilya, the printer whose newspaper had chronicled the city's losses.

She wore ink on her cuffs and carried a bundle of proofs so large that she had tied it to her shoulders with a piece of sailcloth. She explained that the presses were running through the night.

Every household would receive a notice with the questions the assembly intended to ask.

The answers would not bind anyone outside the city, but Ilya wanted the record to show that the citizens had been capable of describing their own future before the Council attempted to describe it for them.

Ilya handed Mira a proof. The text was imperfect. Several paragraphs contradicted one another. A heading had been crossed out and replaced with a question. The last page contained a list of names whose holders had asked not to be counted with either faction.

Mira had never seen an official document admit uncertainty so plainly.

**Mira:** Your notice looks unfinished.

Ilya: Of course it is. We're still alive.

They passed a theater where a company of actors was rehearsing a play for a performance that might never happen. The stage represented the city before the boundary.

One actor carried a bucket of water while another stood at a window and described a harbor that none of the younger company had ever seen.

The audience seats were empty except for a child eating an apple and an old man who appeared to be asleep. The actors continued without embarrassment. Mira asked why they were rehearsing on the night of the assembly.

The director said that if the city was restored, the play would become a history. If the city remained hidden, it would become a fantasy. Either way, the actors wanted to be able to tell it well.

A person did not wait for history to choose the genre of a life before beginning to live it.

Near the old station they met a family packing a cart. The mother was frightened that the city's border would fail without warning and wanted to take her children to the safest district.

Her partner argued that nowhere could be called safe while the machine decided where danger belonged. The eldest child had packed only a box of colored pencils.

He said he planned to draw the sky the day the city opened, so he could remember whether the clouds had looked different from inside.

Mira helped the younger child tighten a strap on the cart. She wanted to offer reassurance. Instead she showed the parents a map of the passages she had seen and marked the routes that appeared stable. She told them what she did not know.

The mother thanked her more warmly than Mira had expected. Perhaps honesty was not the opposite of comfort. Perhaps it was a form of respect that could remain when comfort failed.

At the station gate Mira found a notice nailed beneath the Council's old crest.

It contained a paragraph cut from an early agreement and concealed from the public version: THE BENEFICIARIES SHALL NOT BE REQUIRED TO BE INFORMED OF THE SPECIFIC LOSSES BY WHICH CONTINUITY IS SECURED. The language made her hands cold.

She copied it on the back of Ilya's proof so no official could later claim that ignorance had been an accident.

Cael stopped in front of a row of houses with painted blue doors. A woman stood on a step mending a net. She glanced up as they approached, frowned, and then spoke to him as though he had forgotten an appointment.

The familiarity struck him harder than anger would have. He answered carefully, unwilling to pretend to remember what he could not. She told him that her brother had sailed with Cael on the northern route and had returned once with a broken hand.

Cael had sat through the night making a splint from a piece of broken furniture. The brother had laughed afterward and said that a man who could repair a hand from a chair could probably repair anything.

Cael remembered the chair. It had been painted green. He did not remember the brother's name. The woman supplied it and asked him to repeat it. He did, once and then again.

She looked relieved in a way that suggested she had not expected him to know how to receive a gift without pretending it came from him.

The woman asked whether they would restore the city. Cael said he did not yet know what restoration should mean. She nodded, as if this were the first answer all day that sounded like a person speaking rather than an institution making a promise.

A clock began to strike. It was the same backward-running clock they had seen near the bakery. A boy on a ladder was adjusting its mechanism with a tool too large for his hand.

Each strike sounded a little different, and every citizen in the street paused to count it, perhaps to decide whether it was now one hour earlier or one hour nearer the end.

The boy called down that the clock would not be repaired until the assembly agreed on which direction it should run. Nobody corrected him.

Mira had grown up among people who believed time was a problem with a mechanical solution. She had never considered how much of time belonged to agreement: a birthday, an appointment, a closing hour, a promise to return.

The clock could be made to run either way. The people would still have to decide when to meet.

**Cael:** I used to think this place was waiting for me to come back. Now I think it was living without me, and I was missing it.

**Mira:** That may be the hardest part of finding something lost. It has changed while you were trying to remember it.

**Cael:** Have you changed?

Mira looked at the ink on her fingers, the blue button in her pocket, and the small brass whistle whose owner she had never met.

She thought of the woman in the red coat, the families on the coast, the print shop under the bridge, and the Council's signature on an experiment whose human consequences had been called an acceptable margin.

She had left the observatory believing she needed to discover a fact. She was returning to it with a question about who had the right to decide what the fact meant.

**Mira:** I hope so.

They returned to the square as the first benches were set down. Ilya placed her printed questions on the tables and weighed the corners with small river stones. The mayor arrived carrying an empty box in which citizens could leave written objections.

A woman who had once voted for the Council demanded that her name be included among those who supported a negotiated extension. A carpenter who feared the storm asked to speak against opening the passage immediately.

Several younger people disagreed with both and refused to stand with either faction. No one had yet invented a method for reconciling all their wishes.

The machine would have preferred one answer. The square was full of people preparing to give many. Mira watched the lamps being lit one by one and felt something she had not expected: not confidence, not relief, but a kind of readiness to listen.

## The Beacon Watch

The assembly lamps had not yet been lit when the canal began to rise. At first the water moved only an inch, enough to make the floating candles brush the underside of the bridge. Then the current reversed.

Barges that had been moored against the east bank pulled toward the west, their ropes tightening until the posts groaned. A fish seller shouted that the canal had done this once before, on the night the boundary closed.

Someone ran to fetch the waterworks engineer. Someone else began carrying crates to higher ground. The market changed from an argument into a place where people had to keep each other alive.

Mira watched the surface for several seconds before recognizing the pattern. The water was not flooding from a river. It was moving toward the lens, drawn by the same pressure that had begun pulling the observatory out of its foundations.

The city's walls were being asked to absorb a force they had never been designed to hold.

The Council's agreement had kept the pressure hidden for so long that the people who lived beside the canal had come to think the water's strange habits were ordinary weather.

Ilya appeared with her arms full of printed notices. Her proofs were wet. She handed the driest copies to children, who carried them door to door and shouted directions to the high streets.

The carpenter from the previous evening organized a line of people passing sandbags from the warehouse to the low bridge. A baker brought warm loaves and broke them into pieces because those working on the wall had no time to sit down.

Nobody had elected a leader for the work. Several people began giving orders at once, and the orders were sometimes contradictory, but the wall rose because the workers could see the water and amend the instructions as it moved.

**Mira:** The pressure is going to find the weakest part of the boundary. We need to know where that is.

**Cael:** The eastern sluice. It was built for a much smaller current. If it fails, the hospital district will flood before anyone can move the patients.

Mira asked how he knew. He said he had sailed the canal before the boundary closed. The memory had returned with the smell of wet rope, incomplete but useful. She did not ask him to prove it.

They ran toward the sluice with three volunteers and a coil of cable borrowed from the theater, where it had held a painted moon above a stage for twenty seasons.

The eastern gate was already bending. Water pressed against the iron plates, and blue light shone through the rivets. A woman in a dark green apron stood ankle-deep in the current with a wrench in one hand.

She introduced herself as Adra, keeper of the pumps. Her assistant had gone to the hospital. She had been trying to release the emergency counterweight, but the controls were older than any engineer still alive. She had a manual.

It was written in the same legal vocabulary that governed the lens. She had spent a lifetime repairing the city's water and had never been taught that the instructions for her oldest machine were a kind of contract.

Mira took the manual while Cael secured the cable to the gate. The emergency release required an operator to state the purpose of the waterworks and designate a permitted recipient of the redirected flow.

The first field had been filled with a single word: CONTINUITY. The second was blank. The system would release water only if the operator agreed that the lives below the sluice could be used to preserve the structures above it.

The blank was not an oversight. The blank was the place where an authority had expected to write the name of a district it was willing to sacrifice.

Adra: Can you make it open without filling that in?

**Mira:** I can refuse the old instruction. I don't know whether it will accept a new one.

Adra slammed her wrench against the iron until sparks rose. She said that if the machine refused to recognize a city whose people were drowning, then the machine could be made to open by force. Cael examined the bolts.

He told her which would fail first and which were supporting the entire gate. The three of them began work. A city that was already tearing itself apart did not owe them the luxury of waiting for a philosophical solution.

The first bolt came free. Water struck the wall above it in a spray of blue droplets. Mira found the manual's page for emergency pressure relief and tore it out. She did not do this out of contempt for books.

She did it because the page had been written so no one could change the instructions without destroying the document that made them binding.

She fed the page into the current, and as it dissolved she spoke the words from memory, replacing the blank field with the names of the districts whose residents had come to help.

The counterweight moved. Not far; perhaps the length of a hand. But enough for Adra to wedge a bar beneath the plate. Cael swung his weight against the cable.

On the third pull the gate opened and the water surged into an unused holding basin. The basin filled with a sound like a hundred doors slamming. The canal dropped by half a foot.

Across the city, people on the sandbag line began to shout that the wall was holding.

A boy ran from the hospital with a message written on the back of a prescription. The patients had been moved upstairs. The water had reached the lower steps but gone no farther. The doctors needed blankets and clean drinking water.

Adra sent her assistant to the public baths, where the reserve boilers could be brought into service.

Ilya handed out the remaining dry copies of her notices and added a line in thick ink: THE EASTERN GATE WAS OPENED BY ITS KEEPERS AND THE PEOPLE WHO CAME TO HELP THEM.

Cael leaned against the wall, his hands bleeding where the cable had cut them. Mira took a strip of cloth from her sleeve and tied it around his palm. He protested that the wound was small.

She said that smaller wounds were the ones people neglected until they became more troublesome than larger ones. He accepted the bandage without pretending it was unnecessary.

Mira examined the manual more carefully after the crisis. The pages were numbered with the same cipher as the Council's hidden calculations. She copied the diagram of the gate and its release mechanism into her ledger.

The instrument in the observatory would obey only certain forms of language, but the waterworks had just demonstrated that physical changes could create room for new language to be recognized.

It was evidence that the final choice might not be limited to the Council's four permitted endings.

The water receded. People began returning tools to their owners, although in the confusion nobody remembered who had brought some of them. The carpenter said they could keep the extra cable for the next flood.

Adra said the sluice was no longer the same machine it had been that morning. It had broken a little and become more useful. Mira felt an unexpected affection for the bent iron gate.

It would never qualify for the Council's definition of an elegant solution. It had saved the hospital district.

When the assembly bell sounded, the workers left the wall in shifts. Some went to the square still wearing wet clothes. Others stayed to watch the basin. A few declined to attend because their children needed supper.

No one accused them of failing to participate in history. Mira crossed the bridge with Ilya's revised notice in her hand.

It was smudged and poorly aligned, and it told the truth about something the people had done together before anyone had agreed on what should happen next.

## The Hall Of Open Questions

The assembly could not begin in the square. Rain had entered through the narrow break in the eastern wall and turned the spaces between the cobbles into a series of small channels.

People who had intended to stand in the open now gathered beneath awnings, carrying wet notebooks and demanding to know whether the meeting would be postponed. The mayor announced that the hall of the former customs house had enough seats for half the attendees.

This produced an immediate argument about which half should be admitted. Mira saw how easily even an ordinary arrangement of furniture could become a decision about who was permitted to count.

Nel the teacher arrived with a group of students carrying folding stools. She said the school had a hall with movable walls and a covered courtyard.

It would accommodate everyone if the audience accepted that some people would sit where they could hear but not see the speakers. A printer from the city offered to reproduce the day's statements on paper for anyone who needed to follow them outside.

Someone suggested that the children could carry notes between the rooms. The children objected that they wanted to listen to the debate themselves. The adults apologized, and a different messenger system was arranged.

Cael found himself at the center of the discussion. As a courier, he knew the routes between the public buildings. He sketched the shortest path through the covered streets, marking the slippery steps and the places where carts could pass.

It was a map designed not to exercise authority but to help people arrive. Mira looked at it with unexpected pride. Her entire childhood had been spent among maps whose makers valued accuracy more than welcome.

Cael's map was accurate, but its first question was where a person might safely walk.

The hall smelled of wet wool and hot metal. At the front stood a long table with five chairs. The mayor had placed herself in the middle.

The other seats were marked for the Council's representative, the trades, the families of the missing, and the people of the outer districts.

A young woman carrying a bundle of petitions looked at the arrangement, placed her bundle on the table, and asked where the children would sit. The mayor replied that children could submit written statements.

The woman said that was not the same as being allowed to ask a question in the room.

Mira watched the mayor think. She could have defended the arrangement as an established procedure. Instead she pushed her own chair to the side and asked the students to select a delegate.

The students spent several minutes arguing over whether a delegate ought to be the oldest, the loudest, or the person who had prepared the most questions. They chose the quietest among them, a girl named Bera who had been taking notes throughout.

Bera accepted only after they agreed that she could leave the chair to consult the others whenever she wanted.

The incident took perhaps ten minutes. It changed the atmosphere of the meeting more than any proclamation could have done. People began to move the benches themselves, making room for neighbors who could not climb the steps.

A man with a cane asked whether the speakers could sit down because he could not stand long enough to follow their gestures. The mayor agreed.

An interpreter moved beside the first row, where she could hear without being required to cross a crowded aisle. None of these arrangements changed the machine. They changed who would be present when the machine's future was discussed.

At the back of the hall a woman in an apron raised a piece of paper. She said her bakery had lost three deliveries in the last week and she wanted to know whether the boundary would make flour disappear next.

An engineer answered that he could not guarantee the continuity of the supply routes. The baker asked whether the Council had been able to make such a guarantee before, when it had closed the passage without telling anyone. The engineer admitted it had not.

Several people applauded. The baker looked irritated by the applause; she had wanted to know how much flour to order.

**Cael:** She doesn't need a victory. She needs a shipping schedule.

**Mira:** Then someone should help her make one.

He went to the baker, borrowed her list of suppliers, and brought it to a group of dockworkers who knew which crossings were still functioning. They made a provisional plan using three different routes.

The plan was not elegant and would probably need revision by morning. It was useful. Mira began to see that the meeting could produce more than a decision about the lens.

It could become a way for the city to practice the kind of public conversation the Council had denied it.

Parel arrived with a wooden crate from the quay. Inside were objects whose owners had asked that they be shown: a boy's schoolbook, a pair of women's gloves, a copper pot repaired many times, and a set of keys tied to a blue ribbon.

He placed the crate beside the table and explained that some people could not speak about the boundary without a thing from the life it had divided. Others had refused to have their objects shown.

He asked the mayor to announce that no one would be compelled to display a memory in order to have a vote.

The Council's representative objected that physical objects could not establish the true location of a vanished district. Parel agreed. He added that a person's grief was not a navigational instrument, and that was precisely why it should not be required to function as one.

The argument drew murmurs of approval and suspicion. Mira recognized the danger in allowing the room to treat sincerity as proof. People could remember incorrectly. They could lie. They could be mistaken about the causes of their losses.

But a mistaken witness could still possess a right to know why a machine had been allowed to act upon their life.

A young apprentice stood and said her father had voted for the original agreement. He was now dead, and the apprentice did not want strangers treating him as a villain for a choice made under conditions they could barely imagine.

Across the aisle, a man whose family had been trapped inside the boundary said the father's vote had consequences regardless of whether he understood them. The apprentice asked whether that meant she should stop loving him.

The man answered that he had never asked her to. They looked at one another across the hall, each holding an argument that was too heavy to be resolved by the other person yielding.

Mira spoke only when the mayor invited her. She said the surviving records showed that voters had not been told the full cost of the agreement.

That fact did not make every individual blameless, but it changed the questions an honest inquiry would need to ask. Who had known? Who had chosen not to find out? Who had attempted to stop the experiment?

Who had learned enough to object and had been prevented from speaking? The room listened. For once she did not have to defend the value of a question that had no simple answer.

Mira laid the relief ministry's censored charter on the table. She showed the room the sentence instructing officials to disclose the harm done to outer districts and the signature approving its deletion.

The document provoked anger, but it also gave the inquiry a specific task: identify the official who ordered the deletion and find the surviving correspondence. The evidence turned an accusation into a set of questions that could be answered.

Bera, the student delegate, asked what would happen if the machine showed a future in which opening the boundary killed people. The Council representative said the meeting would have to choose continuity.

A dockworker asked what would happen if keeping it closed killed people whose names were not on the official charts. Neither man could answer without qualifying his principles.

Bera wrote both statements in her notebook and asked the mayor whether the assembly would be allowed to hear the machine's projections directly rather than accept a summary prepared by the Council.

The mayor said she would ask the Chancellor. Bera said that was not the question. Did the assembly have a right to see the projections? The mayor looked toward the representatives. The families' delegate answered yes.

The trades agreed, on the condition that the engineers explain the limitations of their models. The outer districts' representative insisted that people be told what assumptions had been used.

The Council's representative refused to endorse the proposal but did not claim the room lacked authority to adopt it.

The vote was taken by standing, except for those who could not stand and were invited to raise a hand. Some refused to vote because they thought the question premature. Their refusal was counted separately. The result was not unanimous.

That, Mira discovered, made it more trustworthy. She could name the objections, trace the procedure, and explain why the decision had been made despite disagreement. No one had to pretend that dissent had vanished from the room.

A man in the front row asked what an outsider could know about AsterVale. Mira told him the truth: much less than the people who lived there, and more about the machine's construction than any one person should have been required to learn.

He asked whether she would testify under oath. She agreed, then asked to write the oath herself.

It would bind her to distinguish what she had seen from what she had inferred, and to return any evidence that belonged to another community if its custodian demanded it. The man was surprised that she had added restrictions on her own authority.

Mira was surprised by how difficult it felt to write them down.

Cael returned from the dockworkers with a list of supply routes and placed it beside the baker's notes. Parel had found the owner of one pair of gloves in the crate.

Nel had arranged for the next school day to begin late so children could attend the opening testimony. Around the hall, people were producing arrangements small enough to change when they failed.

The great decision was still ahead, but the meeting was already demonstrating that a city did not have to wait for a perfect answer before it began to govern its own life.

Mira went out onto the covered balcony. Rain pattered on the awning and ran in careful lines along its seams. Cael joined her, bringing two cups of the bitter tea served at the hall's entrance.

They watched the streets below, where volunteers moved lamps and carried the last benches inside. The city was not calm. Its people were frightened, and its foundations were still being pulled toward the lens.

Yet Mira found she was less afraid of their disagreement than she had been of the Council's serene certainty.

**Cael:** They could spend years debating some of this.

**Mira:** I hope they get the chance.

**Cael:** You really have changed.

**Mira:** I used to believe a map was finished when the blank spaces were filled. Now I want to leave room for somebody to correct it.

He gave her his empty cup. She balanced it on the rail beside hers. Below, a child tried to catch raindrops in a bowl and shouted whenever the water overflowed.

Mira understood that the meeting would end, the decisions would be made, and the machine would demand a command long before all the questions could be answered. She also understood that the unanswered questions were not defects to be concealed.

They were obligations the survivors would have to carry into whatever came next.

They returned to the hall as the mayor called the first witness. Bera had brought an extra notebook for the questions the speakers could not answer. She placed it at the end of the table and wrote on the cover: QUESTIONS TO RETURN TO.

The title was small enough for a child to carry. It was large enough, Mira thought, for the future the city was trying to make.

## Assembly

The Emergency Assembly met beneath the open sky, because the Council chamber had become a place where people expected lies to be spoken in comfortable chairs. It was a disorderly meeting.

There were questions from bakers, teachers, pilots, and children who did not understand why grown people had allowed a city to remain hidden without first deciding what would happen when it wanted to return. The mayor began with a prepared speech.

A woman carrying a bucket interrupted to ask why the water was running backward. The mayor put away the speech.

The delegates had argued for an hour over whether the square was a suitable place to decide the fate of seventeen thousand people. The mayor wanted the chamber. The baker wanted a schoolhouse with enough chairs for every family.

The clockmaker said there was no room big enough and that perhaps the city had spent too long believing important decisions required a special kind of room.

In the end they carried benches out of shops and kitchens, arranged them in uneven circles, and left the central fountain uncovered so children could sit on its edge. No one could see everyone, but everyone could see someone.

Mira was asked to describe the machine. She tried to begin with the number of lenses, their alignments, and the mechanism by which a sentence could be given the force of a law of nature.

A woman in a flour-dusted apron interrupted her before the second sentence. Would the bakery still have an oven tomorrow? Mira could not answer. Then a cart driver asked whether his daughters would remember their grandmother. She could not answer that either.

A third question concerned the northern hospital. Was its stairwell likely to vanish if the boundaries changed? At last Mira put down her notes.

**Mira:** I can tell you what the machine is doing. I cannot tell you what it will do after we ask it to behave differently. My father claimed he could make that prediction. The city has spent seventeen years paying for his confidence.

Silence followed. It was not the silence of belief. It was the more demanding silence of people deciding what to do with an answer they did not like.

A surgeon from the eastern clinic stood. He had learned to operate in a city where the weather did not follow the season and children occasionally arrived at the hospital with memories older than their grandparents.

He wanted the walls removed, he said, because medicine depended on exchange: the circulation of knowledge, medicine, and the experience of other people. Yet he had seen too many patients die because a confident authority had called a risk acceptable on their behalf.

He would not let the Council make that calculation again. He did not want safety purchased with secrecy. He wanted danger described honestly, by people who would share it.

The baker asked what such honesty would look like at the end of a long night when frightened people wanted certainty.

A young carpenter answered that perhaps it looked like this: a crowd trying not to interrupt one another, even when the sentence being spoken was unbearable. The baker laughed at him, then apologized, then asked him to continue. Their conversation took six minutes.

The machine had never allowed six minutes for a citizen to change her mind.

Cael stood near the back, holding Ilya's engraving of the ship. Someone recognized the uniform on the deck and asked whether he had been on the last voyage through the boundary. He said yes. Another person accused him of having abandoned the city.

Cael did not deny that he had left. He described the storm that carried him out, the injuries, the first weeks spent searching for a route home, and then the terrible slow loss of the knowledge that home existed.

He could hear anger in the crowd; he let it remain anger. Mira had never seen him refuse the easier comfort of an explanation.

**Cael:** I could have searched harder while I still remembered. That much is mine to answer for. But what happened to the people you sent out afterward was not a failure of their affection.

**Cael:** Someone made it impossible to come back and then called their absence a choice.

A woman in the front row covered her mouth. She had sent a son on a fishing boat ten years earlier. She asked whether Cael knew him. He did not. She asked whether that meant the boy was dead. He could not say.

There was no answer large enough for the space between them. Cael came down from the step and sat beside her until the next speaker began.

Mira was beginning to understand that a public decision might not give anyone relief. It could give them something more difficult: a burden that was genuinely theirs, shared out under conditions they could inspect. The Council's bargain had promised to remove that burden forever.

What it removed instead was the right to carry it.

Orsol appeared in the eastern lens, enormous as a mountain and as carefully composed as a portrait in a government building. She offered another generation of safety in exchange for the atlas and its evidence. The citizens could return to their work.

The outer islands could continue to imagine themselves innocent of the agreement. The toll would be paid by places selected according to mathematical criteria. Nobody would need to know which places until after they were gone.

A child asked whether anyone would remember his birthday. Orsol said the question was sentimental. A hundred people began to speak at once.

Mira had once believed that the opposite of secrecy was disclosure. In the market she learned that disclosure was only the beginning. Facts placed in the hands of people who could not act on them were another form of decoration.

The people needed time, language, and the knowledge that their votes could change something. They had been denied all three.

Mayor: We have seventeen thousand residents. The time remaining would not allow a ballot taken from every household. We can send three delegates, elected here and witnessed by the outer world, to speak for the city. But their decision must be limited.

No one has the right to make an irreversible bargain that the living cannot revise.

**Mira:** That is exactly the principle the Council refused to put in the contract.

Orsol: A city cannot vote to undo the laws of physics.

**Mira:** It can vote to stop calling your decision a law of physics.

The Chancellor's face tightened. Mira realized how rarely anyone had addressed her without first asking permission to speak.

Orsol looked older than she had when Mira was a child, and yet the same in the precise way a clock looks the same after years of measuring other people's lives.

Elian stepped onto the fountain. She was young, and the crowd fell quiet not because youth gave her authority but because the people understood she had lived the consequence of a decision made before she could consent.

Elian: When I was little, I was told that my brother had chosen to leave. Later I was told I had imagined him. For years I was ashamed of remembering somebody who supposedly didn't exist.

I have lived here all my life and I love this city. I don't want to lose it. But if keeping it means teaching another girl that her brother never loved her, I don't want that bargain to continue in my name.

Cael stood below the fountain with both hands closed at his sides. Mira saw him trying not to make the moment about his grief. She admired him for it more than she could have explained. Elian had spoken for herself.

He had not interrupted to provide a better version of her story.

A teacher spoke about the schools and the libraries that would be lost if the cities collapsed back into the outside world. A gardener spoke about the children who had been born inside the boundary, who did not remember any other sky.

A pilot argued that the storm might be navigable if it could be studied rather than feared. A mother asked why the choice had to be between an everlasting prison and a catastrophe that killed everyone.

That was the question the machine did not know how to answer. Its terms were binary: keep the world divided or pay the debt by reunifying it. The instrument had no provision for a third way.

The Council had built a government around that limitation, and for seventeen years no one had been permitted to question whether the limitation belonged to the universe or merely to the instrument.

Mira remembered her father's letter. He had written that he had failed to make the erasure gentle. He had not written that he failed to make it reversible. The distinction felt intentional.

A person who knew how to build a device sometimes left the most important part of the instructions in the form of an apology.

She asked the delegates to wait while she returned to the eastern lens. The mayor objected that they had little time; Orsol objected that time had already run out. Mira answered that neither of them would know until someone asked the machine.

Cael volunteered to accompany her.

The delegates were chosen. There was a representative of the families, a representative of the occupations that kept the city alive, and a child chosen by the other children to speak about the future they expected to inhabit.

Mira was relieved they had not chosen her. She had come to give the hidden city a voice, not to acquire one more place where hers counted most.

The sky above the market bent into a pale ring. The atlas began pulling at its brass hinges, as if a great tide had formed between its pages. The closing had begun earlier than predicted.

Orsol's projection faded, and in the silence that followed Mira could hear the frightened breathing of the people around her.

**Cael:** We have to go now.

**Mira:** Then we go with what they've told us. No other story will be accepted as their answer.

They returned through the lens carrying the elected delegation's instructions and the knowledge that the people of AsterVale had already begun deciding what to do with a future that had been withheld from them. Mira kept thinking of the child's question about birthdays.

The sky was full of grand instruments. None of them could tell her whether anyone would remember the next birthday.

## The People At The Gate

After the assembly, the delegates did not immediately follow Mira to the lens. They had households to inform, tools to collect, and arguments still being made in the streets.

The people had agreed on the right to decide; they had not agreed on every consequence of their decision. The mayor asked whether they could take another hour.

Mira could see the pale ring over the city closing by degrees and knew the machine was unlikely to grant one. She said they could take the time needed to make the instructions truthful, but that no delay would be free of risk.

The mayor thanked her for not calling the risk imaginary.

Three delegates had been chosen, and each came to the gate carrying something different. The families' representative carried a book of names with blank lines left for people who had not yet been found.

The representative of the city's trades brought a list of food stores, medicines, ships, and working machinery, because a city returned to the world would need more than the right to exist.

The child chosen by the children carried a small tin box of drawings. One showed the observatory as a house built on clouds. Another showed AsterVale as a circle surrounded by doors.

The last contained no picture at all, only a sentence written with great care: PLEASE DO NOT MAKE US PROMISE TO BE BRAVE.

Mira read it twice. She thought of Tessa from the weather station, of children in the hospital, of the boy who had tried to make the backward clock run again.

Adults were fond of demanding courage from children after making the conditions in which courage became necessary. She folded the drawing and put it back in its box. The child watched until she had closed the lid.

At the boundary, a line of Council officials waited under a canopy of blue silk. Their leader was a man Mira had seen at public lectures, where he introduced the Chancellor with speeches about responsible governance. He carried an order bearing Orsol's seal.

By authority of the first agreement, he declared, no citizen was permitted to leave the hidden city while the system remained unstable. The order used the same words with which the Council had once described the original confinement as voluntary.

The families' delegate stepped forward. She asked whether the official had received permission from the people who lived in AsterVale. He said that the agreement had been made before the birth of most current residents.

She asked whether the people who had been born under it were permitted to reject it. He replied that such matters were beyond his jurisdiction.

Then, with the expression of a man who believed he had said something decisive, he asked them to return to their homes.

**Cael:** You have come to a place where the people are arguing about their own future. You may join the argument. You cannot end it by pointing at a paper no one here was allowed to read.

The official looked at Cael's scar and paled. He recognized a mark he had never expected to see outside a Council archive. He demanded that Cael submit to an inspection.

Cael held out his hand, not in surrender but to make the mark visible to everyone. Several people in the crowd stepped closer. They wanted to know what it meant that a courier had been made part of the machinery of their captivity.

The official's answer was a long explanation about custodial authority. Someone laughed. The laughter spread, not because the subject was amusing, but because the language was so far removed from the lives it claimed to govern.

The official ordered his guards to block the passage. They hesitated. One guard had grown up in the hidden city and recognized the woman standing beside the families' delegate as his aunt.

Another saw the hospital workers carrying blankets through the crowd and stepped out of their way. The order had been written for men who would act like pieces on a board.

The men were human beings standing in a street where other human beings knew their names.

Mira took the old agreement from her satchel. She did not tear it up.

She read its most important clause aloud, including the portion the Council had never published, the clause that said the people benefiting from the bargain need not be informed of the losses. She let the words settle over the crowd.

The official's face remained composed, but he had to lower his eyes to the seal in his hand. The seal was still valid under the rules of the machine. It was no longer convincing to the people standing in front of him.

**Mira:** You may take this paper back to the Chancellor. Tell her the city has read it. Tell her the people whose names it did not include have something to add.

The guard who had recognized his aunt moved aside. Others followed. The official said they were disobeying a lawful order. The aunt said she had a lawful duty to return a borrowed kettle and that the guard could come to supper afterward.

The absurdity broke the remaining tension. The passage cleared a little at a time, not because someone had defeated the Council but because enough people refused to make the Council's authority the only thing in the street.

Elian appeared beside Cael. She had brought a piece of the blue scarf from the bakery, the one that held a memory of him returning for her when they were children.

She asked him whether he would remember her after he crossed the lens again. He told her that he did not know. Then he took the scarf and tied it around his wrist above the compass scar.

**Cael:** If I forget, this will be the beginning of the story when you tell it to me again.

Elian: And if the story changes?

**Cael:** Then you can tell me what I got wrong. I would like to be corrected by someone who was there.

She touched his bandaged hand. For a moment Mira saw the two siblings not as symbols of the passage's cruelty but as people recovering the ordinary awkwardness of having grown older without each other. Elian asked whether Cael still hated pears.

He said he had never liked them. She laughed and told him he had once eaten six in a row to win a bet. He could not remember the wager. He accepted the humiliation with more grace than he had accepted the Council's seal.

The delegates stepped into the passage. The city moved behind them: lanterns being put away, water being pumped from the lower streets, children arguing about whether the sky on the other side would contain the same stars. Not everyone followed.

Many stayed to tend the places they had built. Mira understood that their decision to remain was as important as the delegates' decision to leave. The right to return had to include the right not to be moved against one's will.

The passage trembled. AsterVale's lamps blurred against the blue glass. For one dreadful instant the families' book became transparent, and the written names shone through its pages like fish beneath ice. Mira pressed her hand against the cover and spoke the first name aloud.

Cael spoke the second. The trades' delegate spoke the third. Other people took up the recitation until the book grew solid again. The child with the tin box listened, then began saying the names of her classmates. Some of the names were repeated.

Nobody objected.

They reached the observatory side carrying imperfect instructions, frightened people, and a box of drawings that a machine had never been built to understand. The assembly was over. The work it had begun was not.

## The Road Before Morning

The passage back to the observatory was no longer a stable corridor. Its edges had begun to fray into light, and the objects suspended along its sides moved as though the wind of a different world were blowing through them.

Mira could see the market behind her, the library ahead, and between them a narrow road made from fragments of streets that had once connected the cities. Some fragments were real stone; others were remembered cobbles, transparent enough to show the clouds below.

The delegates crossed slowly, carrying their instructions and the child's box of drawings.

At the midpoint a section of road vanished. The families' representative had just stepped onto it. Cael caught her arm before she fell, but the book of names slipped from her hands and opened in the air. Pages scattered across the passage.

Mira reached for the nearest, then stopped. The road was breaking at a pace that would not allow them to gather everything. She had to choose between the paper and the person. The choice should have been obvious.

The machine had trained her to fear the loss of records so deeply that for one terrible second she felt the temptation to reach for the book first.

She caught the representative's other hand. Cael pulled them both onto solid stone. The pages drifted away, each carrying a name toward the pale sky. The child with the drawings began reciting the names she knew. The trades' delegate joined her. Others followed.

The pages stopped falling. They hung in the air and turned, one after another, toward the voices that called them.

Mira realized that the machine's hold on written memory was weakening when living people refused to let the writing become the only place a name could exist.

**Mira:** Keep speaking. Don't try to make the names perfect. Just say the ones you know.

**Cael:** The road is forming under us. Every time someone speaks, another stone comes back.

They crossed by moving from one restored stone to the next. The families' representative wept as she recovered the book. She said she had been afraid that losing the pages would mean losing the people.

Mira told her that the pages mattered, but the names were not owned by the paper. The representative nodded and held the book against her chest. The lesson had cost them more fear than Mira wished it had.

She could not pretend it was therefore less valuable.

At the far end of the corridor stood the Keeper of the Threshold, the small figure in the silver coat. Its face had aged since Mira last saw it. Fine lines crossed its cheeks, and its hands trembled when it reached for the doorframe.

The Keeper said that the system was exhausting the memories from which it had been built. It could no longer hold the old agreement in place without taking more from the living. The Council's revisions had delayed the collapse but could not prevent it.

The time for a choice had arrived.

Mira asked whether the Keeper had ever wanted to leave the passage. The figure considered the question. It said it had not been designed to want anything.

Then it added that it remembered watching people cross the boundary and sometimes wishing they would turn back before the toll was taken. The admission seemed to surprise it. Its hands became still.

Perhaps the machinery had not anticipated a witness capable of recognizing that a person ought to be warned even when warning was not part of the instructions.

The Keeper led them to a door made from pieces of different buildings. One panel came from a ship's cabin, another from a schoolroom, another from a house whose address had been erased. The handle was a brass star.

Mira recognized the design from her mother's paper constellations. She had thought the star was a private symbol invented for a child.

Now she wondered whether it had been a sign used by people who had tried to create passages the Council could not control.

Cael touched the handle. His scar answered with a blue pulse. The door opened onto the observatory's western gallery, where the floor had begun tilting toward the lens. A few librarians were moving books away from the lower shelves.

They looked up when the delegates entered and immediately began asking where the city representatives should be seated. The ordinary concern for chairs made Mira almost laugh.

The world was at risk of being rewritten, and someone was still trying to arrange a meeting properly.

She asked the librarians to bring a table into the center of the gallery and lay the evidence upon it. The delegates placed their instructions beside the atlas. The child set her box of drawings in the empty space.

Cael put the old watch down near the edge and looked at Mira. He said they had collected enough paper to build a second library.

Mira told him they would probably need a second library once the cities were connected again, because nobody should be able to hide the records of the first in a single room.

The Keeper remained at the door. It said that the machine would soon stop recognizing any authority except the one last entered into its core. Mira understood that Orsol had prepared for this.

The Chancellor would attempt to make the existing agreement permanent by using the emergency to end the possibility of revision. The system had been designed to treat such a command as lawful if it came from the correct seal.

The people standing in the gallery would have to answer with a different kind of authority before the bell finished ringing.

Mira found the missing clause from the first agreement in the documents they had carried. She placed it at the top of the pile, where anyone entering the room would see it before the calculations. It was not a dramatic gesture.

It was a decision about the order in which evidence should be read. For the first time she understood how much power lay in the arrangement of a table.

Cael walked to the window. Outside, the clouds had begun moving in several directions at once. A bridge appeared between two floating islands, then vanished. The stars were visible through the storm, their positions shifting as the lens recalculated the world.

Cael said he had seen weather like this only once before, on the voyage that took him away from AsterVale. The difference was that he now knew where he wanted to return.

Mira joined him. She could hear the bell beginning to ring in the eastern chamber, each note stretching longer than the last.

She thought of her father standing at the same window seventeen years earlier, believing he could make a decision so perfect it would never need to be questioned. She had spent her life trying to become a better version of his certainty.

The night had taught her to want something else: the courage to be corrected by people whose lives she could not fully understand.

They turned from the window and went toward the lens. Behind them the librarians continued moving books, the delegates continued discussing their instructions, and the child continued guarding the box of drawings. The world was still full of ordinary work.

Mira hoped it would remain so after the bell stopped.

## The Station For Things Not Yet Lost

The last approach to the observatory passed through an abandoned passenger station. Its timetable had been painted directly onto the walls, where the columns of destinations climbed from floor to ceiling.

Long ago trains had carried families between the upper islands and the lower harbors, following the same brass guide rails that now supported the great lenses. When the boundary closed, the railway had been declared unsafe.

The tracks remained, but no train had crossed them for seventeen years. Someone still swept the platform every morning. Dust lay only where the roof had begun to leak.

Mira stopped beneath the old clock. Its hands were missing, like those on Cael's watch. The stationmaster's booth was lit by a single lamp. Behind the glass a small figure moved, placing a book on the counter and turning its pages with great care.

Mira expected another ghost from the machine, an apparition prepared to tell her something useful at the cost of a memory. The figure came to the window, lifted the lamp, and revealed an elderly woman with ink on her cheek.

She asked whether they had come for the last train.

Cael said there was no train. The woman agreed. She had been saying so every day since the closure. Then she asked whether he wanted to buy a ticket anyway. He asked where it would take him.

She replied that the destination was not the important part. A ticket was a record that someone had intended to go somewhere, and intentions were sometimes all the authorities left behind when a journey was interrupted.

The woman's name was Edda. She kept the station because her husband had been a driver on the last service. He had entered the boundary with seventy passengers and never returned. The Council had described the disappearance as an unavoidable interruption of transit.

Edda had spent the next seventeen years recording the names of people who arrived asking about missing journeys. Some were relatives of the passengers. Others had been traveling themselves and could no longer remember where they had meant to go.

She had never charged anyone who could not pay.

Mira looked around the booth. Shelves held thin books, each marked with a year and a direction. She opened one.

The entries were modest: a family trying to reach a wedding, a gardener bringing seedlings to his daughter, a boy who had missed the train because he stopped to watch a street performer. The entries preserved travel plans that had never been completed.

Between the columns Edda had written little notes about the people who came to ask. Some were angry. Some were relieved to discover that somebody else remembered the service. Some came back each year even after they had stopped expecting the passengers to return.

Edda: The Council offered me a pension if I would close the booth. I told them a pension was a payment for finishing a job. My job wasn't finished.

**Cael:** What would finishing it look like?

Edda: I don't know. Perhaps it would look like a train. Perhaps it would look like telling a child that a journey ended and that we are allowed to mourn it. But I need to be here to know which.

The answer unsettled Mira. Her own father's disappearance had left a station of the mind in which she had waited for an arrival she could not describe.

She had spent years filling the waiting room with research, refusing to ask whether she might be waiting for an apology rather than a person.

Now the machine offered the possibility of bringing missing places back, and she was afraid that even a perfect restoration would leave something unresolved in the old rooms of her life.

Cael asked to see the register for the year of the closure. Edda showed him the first page, which had been repaired with clear paper around its edges. The passengers were numbered. Beside each number stood a name and a destination.

Some names had been crossed out by the Council. Others appeared only as faint depressions in the page, as if someone had written them and the ink had been drawn away. Halfway down the list Cael found a surname he recognized. VEY.

There were two people registered under it, an adult and a child. The adult's first name had been erased. The child's name was still legible: LINA.

Cael did not speak for a long time. Mira knew the pressure of a fact becoming a possibility. The name might belong to his family. It might belong to someone entirely unrelated. The name could answer one question and leave a hundred others.

Edda offered to turn the book toward the light. Cael nodded. In the margin beside the child's name was a description of luggage: one trunk, two small bags, and a parcel wrapped in oilcloth.

The parcel was to be held at the quay for the passenger's return.

**Cael:** I saw that parcel. It's still there.

Edda: Then somebody kept the promise. That matters.

**Mira:** Did the train come back at all?

Edda: The engine did. It arrived three days later with no driver and no passengers. The doors opened at every station, though nobody was aboard. We inspected it for damage. We found a little shoe under the back seat.

I took it to the quay with the other lost things.

Cael closed his eyes. He had seen the shoes. He could now imagine a child who had worn them, though the image was uncertain and might have been assembled from the information he had just learned.

He asked Edda whether she remembered anything about Lina. The stationmaster said the girl had been restless and frightened of the dark, and that her companion had promised to tell her a story when the train entered the long tunnel.

Edda remembered this because she had heard the promise from the platform and had thought it kind.

**Cael:** I have spent years believing I was the one abandoned. What if I was the one who failed to come back?

**Mira:** The register says they boarded the train. It doesn't say what happened after. Don't make the missing part of the record into a verdict against yourself.

He opened his eyes. He asked Mira to repeat the sentence. She did. He said he wanted to remember it, not because it absolved him, but because he had been making the opposite assumption for so long that it felt like an unquestioned fact.

Edda gave him a blank ticket. He wrote LINA VEY on one side and on the other: FIND OUT WHETHER SHE WANTED TO RETURN. Then he put the ticket beside the receipt from the quay.

Mira looked at the register again. Two pages later she found her father's signature, entered as a guest inspector for the same last service. The signature had been written beside a note: requested authority to delay departure; request denied.

Mira felt the old anger return so quickly that it almost blotted out the information. Her father had known something was wrong. He had tried to stop the train. He had also continued working for the people who would send it into the boundary.

The page made him neither innocent nor irredeemable. It gave her another event to place beside the others, another chance to understand the limits of what she had been told.

Edda: Your father argued with the transport director. I heard him say the instruments could not guarantee a return. The director said the risk was acceptable. Your father asked whose risk he was accepting. I thought that meant the service would be delayed.

I was wrong.

**Mira:** Did he try to stop the engine?

Edda: I don't know. I saw him walk toward the rear carriage. Then the doors closed. That's the part I witnessed. Anything after that would be a guess.

Mira thanked her. The discipline of Edda's answer mattered. She had not transformed uncertainty into comfort. She had refused to invent a heroic final act for Mira's father merely because a daughter stood asking about him.

Mira touched the page where his name had been written. For the first time she felt able to acknowledge that she might never learn the last thing he thought.

A future in which that remained unknown was not necessarily a future in which every other truth was inaccessible.

The stationmaster brought them to a little room behind the platform. Its walls were covered with tickets people had left voluntarily, each bearing a destination they wished to reach someday. Some had been written by children.

Others had been left by the elderly in shaking hands. A few named places that existed only on the old charts. One simply read HOME, with a date seventeen years in the past. Edda had never sorted the tickets by importance.

They hung together like the leaves of a small tree, their edges moving whenever the door opened.

They sat for a few minutes on the platform while Edda made tea. The last light of evening fell through the broken roof in narrow bands. Cael looked at the rails disappearing into the tunnel.

He said he had never been able to decide whether the most terrible part of his amnesia was losing the people he loved or losing the knowledge that he had loved them. Mira answered that the second loss made the first harder to mourn.

Without the name of the feeling, a person could mistake grief for a flaw in their own character.

**Cael:** If Lina is still alive, I have to find her.

**Mira:** Yes.

**Cael:** If she doesn't want to see me?

**Mira:** Then you'll have to find a way to honor that. But first we have to make a world in which she can tell you.

The sentence settled between them. It was another small revision to the language of rescue. Bringing a person back was not the same as having a claim upon them. Opening a city did not mean its citizens were obliged to return.

Restoring a memory did not make the remembered person available to the one who remembered. Mira wondered whether the machine could be taught so subtle a distinction.

If it could not, perhaps the distinction would have to live in the people who inherited the machine's work.

Edda insisted they take two blank tickets for the journey ahead. She stamped the backs with the station's old mark, which showed a train crossing a bridge above the clouds. The tickets bore no destination. She said that was how they were now issued.

People could write the destination themselves, and they could change it if they needed to. The Council had once called this practice disorderly. Edda called it the first honest timetable in seventeen years.

At the far end of the platform they found a second departure board, half hidden behind a fallen curtain. Its destinations were not place-names but verbs: REMEMBER, RETURN, REMAIN, REFUSE. The letters had been painted over many times. Their edges were thick with color.

Mira thought of the four outcomes the lens had begun to display, and she wondered how much of the final choice had been made before anyone living had a chance to speak. She copied the words into the atlas but did not circle one.

When they left, Edda returned to her booth and resumed entering the day's visitors in her register. She wrote their names, the hour, and the fact that they had not bought passage. The platform lamp burned behind them as they climbed the observatory stairs.

Cael carried the receipt, the ticket, and a little more knowledge of what he might have lost. Mira carried evidence that her father had once attempted to stop a departure he had helped make possible. Neither object promised an easy resolution.

Both gave them something more useful than certainty: a reason to let other people tell the remainder of their own stories.

## Return To Lens

The observatory was no longer stable. Its floors had developed a slow inclination toward the eastern lens, as if the building had begun sliding down a slope no architect had calculated. Doors opened on rooms they did not lead to.

A staircase from the music hall appeared in the star library; the maintenance corridor ended at a window through which Mira could see her childhood kitchen. Her mother was putting salt on the sill. The scene lasted one heartbeat.

Then the window looked out on lightning again.

Before entering the control chamber, Mira returned to the little room where she had slept as a child. Its door had reappeared between two stone columns as the building shifted. The room was smaller than she remembered.

This was almost a relief; memory should have its ordinary embarrassments as well as its terrors. The bed was still beneath the window, and a paper constellation hung from the ceiling on a thread of blue silk.

Her mother had cut the stars from the backs of rejected Council documents. The shapes were imperfect. One had six points and another nine.

Mira had been very young when her mother explained that the number of points did not matter if the child who named a star knew how to find it again.

Cael stopped at the doorway. The shifting architecture seemed to have made him cautious of crossing any threshold without an invitation.

Mira remembered how he had stood outside the west door on the night of his arrival, offering a letter from a vanished city without demanding that she accept it. The gesture had seemed almost theatrical then. Now it was the beginning of a pattern.

He did not want to become one more person who made decisions for her while pretending the decisions were gifts.

**Mira:** I used to hide here when the Council held its dinners. They would bring guests upstairs to look at the telescopes and speak about the world as though the world were a property they administered.

**Mira:** My father used to bring me a plate of cakes and tell me to stay quiet until the speeches were over.

**Cael:** You must have been an unusually obedient child.

**Mira:** I was excellent at staying quiet while making elaborate plans to ruin something later.

She lifted the paper constellation. Written on the back of the largest star were three words in her mother's handwriting: LET HER CHOOSE. The instruction had no obvious subject except Mira herself.

She thought of her father, who had tried to keep the world safe by denying it choices, and her mother, who had left a child's toy with an unfinished sentence about freedom. Love could be protective. It could also be afraid.

A choice was not a promise that nothing would hurt. It was an agreement not to borrow another person's life without asking.

She tucked the star inside the atlas. It left a pale outline on the blank page, as though the paper were considering where to put it. Cael watched the shape appear but said nothing.

They stood together long enough for the room to remember the people who had lived in it. Then the door began to vanish behind them, and they returned to the control chamber by the corridor that still existed.

Cael carried the atlas under one arm and the delegation's sealed account under the other. The paper had grown heavy.

Every word it contained represented something the machine's old agreement did not know how to hold: a right to decide, a right to revise, a right to change one's mind even after a safe choice had become a dangerous tradition.

Mira took the charred contract if she had it, or the names, or the witness's clapper, and placed it beside the atlas. The three records had not become more complete during the journey. But the missing parts now had witnesses in the world.

That mattered more than the machine expected.

She examined the base of the eastern lens. Behind a panel bearing her father's name she found a second mechanism: an unfinished ring of twelve slots, one empty, eleven filled with little glass bottles. In each bottle floated a word.

She could read some of them. Mercy. Silence. Debt. Arrival. Home. The final bottle, smallest and nearest the empty slot, contained the word Again.

**Mira:** It's a grammar.

**Cael:** I beg your pardon?

**Mira:** The instrument isn't just a device. It constructs a statement about the world. We are living in the conclusion of a sentence written seventeen years ago. The missing slot is where the sentence can be changed.

She opened her father's maintenance notes. Between diagrams of gears were fragments of an abandoned language in which a command became binding only when spoken by two parties who understood the same word differently.

Her father had tried to design an opening clause that would allow the agreement to be renewed by those who were excluded. The Chancellor had forbidden him to complete it.

**Cael:** What would filling the slot do?

**Mira:** Give the city the chance to write a new clause. But the machine has to recognize the voices. It recognizes my father's blood, your scar, and the delegates' authority. It might accept us as witnesses if we can bring them to the lens.

**Cael:** The delegates are at the market. We can fetch them.

**Mira:** And Orsol will be at the eastern stair when the bell strikes. If she reaches the control ring first, she can lock the clause again. We may not have time to do both.

Cael looked toward the door. The stonework of the observatory shook. A coil of copper fell from the ceiling.

He caught it before it struck the atlas, then stared at the metal in his hands as if it had reminded him of something he had been unable to say.

**Cael:** I know this ring. I was here when it was built. I wasn't a child. I was the messenger your father called to carry the first words through the lens. We thought we were saving the cities.

**Cael:** When the toll began taking names, I tried to tell them. They made me forget so that I would keep returning with another message.

**Mira:** You remember now?

**Cael:** Pieces. I remember carrying a letter addressed to you when you were seven. I remember Elian crying because I could not stay. I remember your father burning the compass into my wrist. He apologized while he did it.

**Cael:** I told him the apology would not matter unless he stopped.

The room grew quiet around them. Mira had often imagined her father as a victim of the Council. Now she saw that his grief had come with responsibilities. He had tried to repair the harm. He had also caused it.

Love and culpability were not opposites; a person could carry both, and the people who loved him could be asked to carry the knowledge without being asked to excuse it.

She looked into the lens. Her mother's remembered figure stood far away in the darkness, not moving. Mira wanted to ask whether her father had ever found a way to forgive himself. She did not.

The question would have been another attempt to make the dead answer for the living.

*Choice: face orsol*

## Face Orsol

Cael hesitated at the doorway. Mira asked him to trust the people of AsterVale to travel without the Council's permission and to return with the delegates. He nodded, but before leaving he placed his old watch in her hand.

Its face was dark blue and had no hands. He had carried it for years because a person without a reliable history sometimes needed a reminder that time continued even when the records did not.

**Cael:** Give it back when we meet on the other side.

**Mira:** And if we don't?

**Cael:** Then it can remind you that promises are not guarantees.

Mira went to the eastern stair. Orsol was waiting beneath the portraits of the first directors, carrying the official seal that could close the lens. The Chancellor did not look surprised to see her alone.

She had expected Mira to choose confrontation, perhaps because Mira had spent most of her adolescence perfecting the art of arguing with people who held power over her.

Orsol: Do you have the atlas?

**Mira:** Yes. I also have the voices of the people you tried to make silent.

Orsol: The people have no idea what they are choosing.

**Mira:** They know they were not asked before. They understand that better than you do.

The Chancellor held out the seal. For the first time Mira saw that Orsol's hand was shaking. Her authority had concealed fear so long that she had almost succeeded in making it a private virtue.

Mira wondered whether the Chancellor had once been a decent person who failed to bear the sight of a disaster and then spent the rest of her life making sure no one could contradict the decision she had made in its shadow.

Orsol: If I am wrong, everything I have done was cruelty.

**Mira:** You don't need me to make you innocent. You need to decide what you will do now.

The seal lost its blue light. Orsol lowered it, and for one moment she looked like a tired woman standing in an empty corridor while a building she loved came apart around her.

## The Last Unwritten Clause

The eastern lens had grown so large that the machinery around it looked like scaffolding built by children. Its brass rings rotated in opposite directions, and between them the air was full of torn instructions.

Mira could read fragments as they passed: CONTINUITY, NECESSITY, ACCEPTABLE LOSS, PRESERVATION OF THE WHOLE. The words were not lies in themselves. They became lies when the machine treated them as complete sentences and refused to name the people on whom their completion depended.

The Council's Chancellor stood on the far platform. Orsol had removed the ceremonial coat she wore for public appearances. Beneath it she looked older and more tired than the image projected over AsterVale's market. Her sleeves were rolled to the elbows.

A thin line of blood crossed one wrist where the control ring had cut her. She was still holding the official seal, though its blue light had dimmed until it resembled a dying ember.

Orsol: I hoped you would come to negotiate. I see you have brought witnesses.

**Mira:** I brought people. They came because the decision concerns them.

Orsol: People are poor instruments for determining what must be done in a crisis.

**Mira:** Then perhaps the crisis should not have been designed around the needs of an instrument.

Orsol did not answer immediately. She looked past Mira to Cael's scarf and to the delegation standing behind them. The child held her box of drawings against her chest.

Orsol appeared to notice it, and for a moment her expression changed into something like recognition. She asked the child why she had come. The child said that the adults had already made too many decisions without asking what children wished to remember.

Orsol said memory could be dangerous. The child replied that forgetting had not made any of them safe.

The lens responded to the exchange with a low vibration. It had recognized the voices but not their authority. Its grammar was old and precise. To change a world, it wanted to know who was authorized to speak for that world.

The first agreement had given an answer that no living citizen could challenge. Mira's father's unfinished ring had been an attempt to make that answer revisable.

The last empty slot waited beside the control table, shaped like a space where an object should have been laid but never was.

Mira opened her ledger. She put the names of the missing on one side and the Council's calculations on the other. The pages lifted in the wind. She spoke a sentence describing the harm done by the original agreement.

The machine answered by showing the disasters that the agreement had prevented: storms held at the boundary, floods diverted from cities, plagues stopped before they spread. The images were real enough to frighten her. The Council had not invented the danger.

It had used the danger to claim a permission nobody had granted.

**Cael:** We are not saying the danger was imaginary.

Orsol: Then what will you do with the people who die if you remove the wall?

**Cael:** We will remember them. And we will tell the living what we know before we ask them to bear the risk.

Orsol shook her head. She said remembrance was not a defense against suffering. Cael agreed. The simple answer seemed to strike her harder than an argument. She had been trained to meet accusations of cruelty with an account of necessity.

She had not prepared for people who could acknowledge necessity and still refuse the secrecy with which she had wrapped it.

The control platform shook. One of the brass rings cracked along a line of old repairs. Mira saw the dates stamped into the metal: the first test at the ferry landing, the vanished school, the garden, the northern catastrophe, Bellwether, AsterVale.

The system had been patched repeatedly without anyone returning to ask whether its first premise remained defensible. Every repair had made the machinery larger. Every repair had made the people affected by it more distant from the people authorized to operate it.

She thought of the water gate in the city. Adra had not waited for the old instructions to become just. She had turned the bolts and found a way to keep the hospital standing.

The lesson was not that machines could always be made harmless. It was that a person who understood a machine could refuse to let the machine's existing rules exhaust the possibilities for action.

**Mira:** The empty slot is not meant for a new ruler. It is meant for a condition.

Orsol: Which condition?

**Mira:** That no agreement can be made permanent merely by making its victims unable to remember that it exists.

The words hung in the air. The lens attempted to parse them. It showed an error: CONDITION REQUIRES AN AUTHORITY. Mira turned to the delegates.

She asked them to speak the names of the people who had instructed them, not as a spell but as a record of whom they could answer to afterward. The families' representative began with the missing.

The trades' representative named the workers who would have to repair the city. The child named the children who had asked her to carry their drawings. Cael added his own name and Elian's. Mira spoke her father's name, then her mother's, then her own.

The ring moved. One of the old instructions fell away like a flake of burnt paper. Across the glass appeared the outline of a doorway whose frame was made from the names being spoken.

Beyond it, Mira could see a shore under an ordinary sky. The machine had found a way to represent a choice made by people rather than one imposed upon them. It had not yet found a way to ensure the choice would end safely.

The Chancellor approached. She said that if Mira opened the passage, she would be responsible for the suffering that followed.

Mira answered that she would be responsible for the part she chose, including the decision to leave others free to make choices of their own. She would not accept responsibility for every disaster the Council had hidden beneath the word safety.

Neither would she claim innocence merely because she had acted with good intentions.

Orsol looked at the control ring. Her hand was trembling. She had spent the better part of a lifetime believing the world would end if the agreement were changed.

The possibility that the world could survive her loss of authority seemed to frighten her almost as much as the storms. She did not hand over the seal. She did not attempt to stop Mira from approaching the empty slot.

For the first time that evening, she allowed the instrument to wait while someone else spoke.

Mira could feel the choices gathering behind the lens. They were not four easy futures. Each would ask something different of the people who had survived the long concealment. One would open the sky to danger and reunite the cities.

One would require a living witness to keep the passage from closing. One would make a public bargain that could be revised. One would tear up the agreement and leave the world to govern its own broken mechanisms. The machine could show consequences.

It could not decide which consequences a free people ought to choose.

The child with the drawings stepped beside Mira. She placed her tin box on the table and opened it to the picture of AsterVale surrounded by doors.

Beneath the drawing she had written another sentence, smaller than the first: SOME DOORS SHOULD BE HARD TO CLOSE. Mira took the picture and laid it in the empty slot. The brass ring brightened.

A sentence appeared across the lens in letters large enough for the people on both sides to read: THE AGREEMENT MAY BE CHANGED BY THOSE WHO MUST LIVE UNDER IT.

The floor stopped moving for one heartbeat. Mira knew the pause would not last. The old machinery was still failing, the storms still gathering, and the people on both sides still afraid. But the machine had recognized a clause it had never recognized before.

There was now a space in its language where a choice could be made without first erasing the person who would have to live with it.

## Final Engine

The last bell began to ring. It did not stop. Its sound moved through the observatory as a physical force, breaking the remaining windows and lifting pages into the air.

The atlas rose from the table and opened to the blank place where Bellwether had been. Each vanished name appeared for one instant in a halo of blue light, then fell backward into the pages like rain.

The machine's twelve rings bore the marks of all the hands that had turned them.

Here was a dent from her father's instrument, there a gouge where a previous director had tried to force a locked position, and lower down a narrow polished stripe at the height of a child.

Mira put her palm against that stripe and recognized her own scale of motion. When she was seven, she had believed that helping her father turn a wheel meant helping him turn the stars. He had allowed the misunderstanding.

Later he had allowed much worse ones.

The air beyond the lens became transparent. She saw the upper islands, the steeples of Bellwether, the markets of AsterVale, the little school with maps of impossible bedrooms. She saw a hospital at the coast where people waited beneath a leaking roof.

She saw a grandmother sewing her name into a coat she hoped would outlast her. The machine had reduced all these things to a system of values. It had numbers for loss, continuity, risk, and projected suffering.

Mira had learned to admire numbers because they could reveal what a human eye would miss. She still admired them.

But numbers did not tell her who had been allowed to define what counted as suffering, or what another person might willingly risk to keep the life that belonged to them.

Cael took his repaired watch from his pocket. It still had no hands. He set it on the control ring where the Council's seal would once have rested. Its empty face showed the reflection of Mira's own.

Neither of them could measure how much time remained. The bell made measurement impossible, each stroke arriving before the previous one had faded.

She thought of the dockmaster's register, the printer's proofs, the child's mechanical bird, the doctor's refusal to call an unknown danger acceptable on somebody else's behalf. None of those witnesses had offered a perfect plan. Together they had made a different kind of map.

**Mira:** I thought my task was to restore the missing places. Then I thought it was to punish the people who took them. Now I think both were too small. A place cannot belong to a ledger, even a truthful one.

**Mira:** It belongs to the people who have to wake up there tomorrow.

**Cael:** And if there is no tomorrow?

**Mira:** Then they should at least have been asked what they wished to do with tonight.

The eastern lens expanded until it filled the room. AsterVale became visible behind it, enormous and impossibly near. Mira could see the market square, the House of Returns, the backward clock, and Elian's blue scarf moving through a crowd.

The city was no longer something she could place on a map. It was a place full of people asking to be allowed to remain real.

Orsol stood beside the main control ring. She had the official seal in one hand, but its light had faded. The delegates, or their written instructions, had reached the observatory.

Their words appeared around the lens, not as a single decision but as several demands: the right to remain, the right to leave, the right to remember, the right to refuse a rescue that was indistinguishable from imprisonment.

**Cael:** It's listening.

**Mira:** No. It is waiting for someone to give it a command. We need it to understand that listening is different.

On the far side of the glass stood the Keeper of the Threshold. It wore the same small silver coat, and its face was older than any face should be allowed to become while still resembling a child.

It pointed to the empty slot in the twelvefold ring.

Keeper: The first agreement was a statement about what must be lost. The new one must be a statement about what can be shared. But every statement has a cost. Someone will have to teach the machine a word it has never understood.

Mira looked down at her father's watch in Cael's hand, at the ledger of names, at the page of the contract or the clapper of the witness's bell. None was a perfect tool.

All had become useful because someone had refused to allow them to be the last word.

The lens displayed four outcomes. In the first, the cities rejoined the world and the storm was allowed to pass through them, though not without danger. In the second, a living witness entered the instrument and became the memory that held the passage open.

In the third, the Council's agreement was renewed for one final generation under public supervision, preserving the world as it was while the citizens prepared for a safer return.

In the fourth, the instrument was destroyed; no bargain could be made again, and the world would have to bear both the missing places and the responsibility for finding them by ordinary means.

**Cael:** Which of those is the right choice?

**Mira:** I don't know. I used to think the purpose of studying the stars was to learn enough that I would never have to say that. Now I think the stars have been trying to teach me how to say it properly.

Orsol raised her face toward the lens. The Chancellor had spent her life believing that a person in authority was obliged to be certain. She looked at the four possibilities and, for the first time, did not announce that one of them was necessary.

Orsol: The city has given us its conditions. What do you propose to write into the final clause?

Mira looked at Cael. He looked back without attempting to choose for her. The atlas was open, its blank places waiting. Beyond the windows the first light of an unfinished morning touched the clouds.

Mira understood then that an ending was not a place where a story ceased. It was a place where the living accepted that the consequences of their choices would outlast the sentences in which they described them.

*Choice: ending covenant*

## Ending Covenant

Mira did not choose to open the sky that morning. Nor did she accept the Chancellor's request to preserve the old agreement for another generation without alteration. She asked the delegates for a third kind of clause: a temporary covenant, recorded in public, lasting

only until the next solstice, during which the cities would remain held apart but the people could cross freely, their histories could be shared, and any resident could challenge the terms in a court chosen by both worlds.

Orsol: You are using an emergency instrument to found a government.

**Mira:** Yes. It is better than using an emergency to excuse the absence of one.

There were objections. The machine had not been designed to distribute its power. The Council lacked the authority to surrender an agreement it had made in secret.

The delegates feared that another delay would become permanent, as delays so often do when the people who benefit from them are allowed to define what counts as a solution.

Mira addressed each objection without pretending any of them was foolish.

She proposed a date that could not be extended without a vote from both cities and the outer islands, a public archive of every payment the instrument had taken, and a right of passage for families separated by the boundary.

She insisted on an accounting of the missing people even if the missing could not yet be returned.

Elian spoke from the eastern lens. She wanted the covenant written in a language that the children of AsterVale could understand. A boy beside her asked whether the document would allow him to visit the sea. The delegates added a clause guaranteeing travel.

A woman whose father had died outside the boundary asked whether it would allow her to visit his grave.

Another clause was added, though the words had to be revised many times before they stopped sounding like a promise the machine might interpret too narrowly.

The atlas recorded each amendment. The Chancellor watched with an expression of genuine dismay as the agreement grew from a page of neat mathematical balances into a document that admitted how many people had been left outside the original calculation.

The new contract was ugly by the standards of elegant law. It was full of exceptions, responsibilities, and rights that required people to speak and listen and argue. Mira trusted it more for that.

The Keeper of the Threshold placed its hand on the empty bottle marked Again. The other bottles opened one by one.

From them came voices: the first directors, their assistants, the families who had been erased to pay the toll, and the people who had signed the original rescue agreement. Their voices disagreed. The Keeper did not silence them.

The covenant became binding only when it had recorded the disagreement along with the decision.

A bridge of light formed between AsterVale and the eastern observatory. It was narrow enough that two people had to turn sideways to pass one another.

From the hidden side came a girl carrying a blue scarf, and from the outer side came a man whose old watch had no hands. They met in the middle and embraced for a long time while the machine continued transcribing the terms.

Mira felt relief, and then suspicion. Temporary solutions had a habit of becoming respectable cages.

She wrote the covenant's expiration in large letters at the top of the first page and repeated it at the bottom: this agreement will end whether or not the Council is ready to admit that it has ended.

**Cael:** That seems very emphatic.

**Mira:** I have been studying bureaucrats all my life. They are excellent at turning temporary discomfort into inherited architecture.

The storm quieted. It did not disappear. Around the boundary the air still carried competing histories, and people crossing the bridge sometimes returned with memories that did not belong to them.

Mira called these conditions risks rather than prohibitions and posted clear notices at every passage. Nobody was forced to cross. Nobody was forbidden merely because crossing might make their life difficult.

The first month was chaos. People arrived with bills addressed to cities that had never appeared on the outside's maps. Families reunited, discovered they had become strangers, and began the slower task of learning one another again. Courts disagreed about property.

Cartographers complained that the boundary moved too much to be drawn. Children insisted on drawing it anyway, usually with dragons in the empty places.

The second month was harder. A ship carrying medical supplies vanished in the storm and reappeared three days later with its crew alive and two passengers unaccounted for. Newspapers demanded that the bridge be closed.

Orsol said the disaster proved her original policy had been necessary. Mira answered that a public government could investigate a disaster without pretending the people harmed had never existed.

The investigation disclosed that a former Council official had tampered with the navigation charts to demonstrate the failure of the covenant. The trial was public.

The accused was permitted a defense, the witnesses were allowed to tell their stories, and the court recorded the incident without deleting inconvenient evidence. Mira considered this an achievement more significant than the recovery of the ship.

On the night before the solstice, people gathered on both sides of the bridge. The covenant was due to expire at dawn.

There had been six months of argument and risk, but there had also been six months in which a child could wake and know that someone in another city remembered her name.

The delegates proposed a permanent public body to oversee the passage and an engineering commission to dismantle the machine's toll mechanism without closing the boundary.

Mira looked at the old atlas. Its blank places had begun filling with notes written in many different hands. It was no longer one scholar's instrument; it had become a record of the questions people asked when they refused to accept a single answer.

The vote took place in the market and in the outer islands at the same hour. The majority approved the new agreement, but a substantial minority opposed it.

The minority's objections were recorded, and the new body was given a legal duty to revisit them. It was an unsatisfying conclusion for people who wanted history to end with a victory parade.

Mira thought it a promising beginning for people who intended to live in history after the parade went home.

Orsol resigned before the vote. She gave Mira a sealed envelope containing the names of the people she had personally ordered erased. The list was incomplete. She did not ask for forgiveness when she handed it over.

Mira could not tell whether that was dignity or shame. The Commission would determine what responsibility required; Mira no longer believed the Chancellor's feelings were the center of the problem.

Cael took charge of the new courier route between the cities. He sent Mira letters from each island, half practical reports and half stories about people he had met. The letters were badly organized, but they arrived.

Mira kept them in a box labeled EVIDENCE OF AN INCONVENIENTLY RELIABLE FRIEND.

At the second solstice, no one remembered whose idea it had been to write the covenant on temporary paper. That was good. The agreement no longer belonged to its authors.

It belonged to the people who revised it, taught it, argued about it, and lived under it. Mira had learned enough about the cost of being remembered to find a certain comfort in becoming less important to the story.

## Epilogue Covenant

The first new bridge was built of ordinary stone. It took eight years, cost more money than anyone had expected, and required engineers from both worlds to agree on a definition of level ground.

When it was finished, the mayor of AsterVale and the governor of the outer islands crossed it from opposite ends. They met in the middle, shook hands, and immediately began disagreeing about the toll for freight wagons.

Mira watched from a bench with a book in her lap. She had written a history of the covenant but left the last chapter blank. The bridge was proof that people could finish a structure without finishing an argument.

She wanted the book to leave room for that distinction.

The first public hearing under the covenant lasted three days. The first day was devoted to the meaning of the word temporary. Several officials argued that a promise intended to last only until the solstice might collapse the moment the solstice arrived.

The bakers pointed out that bread was temporary and that nobody considered this a reason not to make it. A surveyor said the analogy was charming but imprecise. The baker invited him to breakfast and declined to argue with an empty stomach.

The second day concerned the rights of people who wished to leave the hidden city. Some families wanted immediate passage for everyone.

Others feared that the city would be emptied of skilled workers and that the people too poor or ill to travel would be left behind. A teacher proposed a temporary exchange program. The hospital proposed shared supply routes.

The river pilots asked who would maintain the bridges. Each suggestion created a new question. Mira had grown up believing that the purpose of a good system was to reduce the number of questions its users could ask. She began to suspect the opposite.

On the third day, the Council's former Chancellor appeared to testify. Orsol did not ask to be forgiven. She described the early experiments, the weather models, and the choices made after the northern catastrophe.

She tried once to describe the agreement as the least harmful of several terrible possibilities. A woman whose family had been erased interrupted her and asked what had happened to the other possibilities.

Orsol admitted that the records had been destroyed so nobody could argue for them afterward. The silence that followed lasted longer than any speech.

Mira looked at Orsol and remembered the room beneath the lens, the table of brass names, and the place where the machine had demanded that someone fill in the name of a district it was prepared to sacrifice.

She wanted to hate the woman for making the world smaller and then claiming to have preserved it. She did hate some of what Orsol had done. But she could not pretend that hatred would rebuild the villages or determine the weather.

The hearing had to remain a place where testimony could be challenged and a record could be made, not a theater in which Mira's anger became law.

The witnesses worked for months. Their report was long and often contradictory.

It named the people who had died, the towns that had been erased, the storms that had been diverted, and the officials who had signed the first agreement without telling those who would bear its price.

It contained appendices written by people who had disagreed with the main conclusions. Mira insisted that the appendices remain attached. A report that hid its own disagreements would only build the next disaster from more elegant paper.

Cael traveled between the cities carrying copies of the report. He had been a courier before he became an unwilling part of the machine. The work was ordinary again. Some packages were urgent. Others were not.

A woman in Bellwether asked him to carry a birthday card to her sister in AsterVale. He delivered it three weeks late because the winds changed. The sister thanked him and complained about the delay.

He considered the complaint a sign that the world was recovering.

When the first solstice arrived, the sky above the observatory filled with a pale ring. The machine requested instructions about whether to renew the agreement. The representatives met in public.

They argued for two days, amended the terms, and approved a shorter extension with stronger provisions for free travel and shared emergency supplies. Some citizens were disappointed. They had wanted the old system abolished at once. Others believed the extension was reckless.

The decision had an appeal process and a date on which it would be revisited. Neither feature satisfied everyone. Both made it possible to begin again.

Mira kept a calendar above her desk. Every important agreement had a date beside it, not only the day it was signed but the day its authors would have to answer for whether it had worked.

She drew small stars beside the dates of the hearings she most feared. The fear did not disappear. It made her prepare more carefully.

At the eighth solstice, the new stone bridge finally connected the cities without passing through the lens.

Its builders had argued over the type of mortar, the strength of the supports, the height needed for river traffic, and whether the bridge should bear the names of the first negotiators. The last question had caused the most trouble.

Some wanted the names engraved on the parapets; others thought the bridge should belong to the people who crossed it, not to those who signed the papers.

In the end they engraved the names of the bridge workers on the underside, where future inspectors would see them. Mira approved.

On opening day, a small crowd gathered beneath flags from both cities. The children ran across before the officials could complete their speeches.

One child stopped halfway and announced that she had just crossed the border twice, because her mother had told her the border was invisible and she wanted to find out whether crossing it repeatedly made her feel different. It did not.

The mayor suggested she return for the official ribbon cutting. She declined. The ribbon was cut without her.

Cael found Mira by the bench and placed a parcel beside her. It contained a new notebook, bound in blue cloth and stamped with a small eight-pointed star.

On the first page he had written: FOR THE QUESTIONS WE HAVE NOT YET LEARNED TO ASK. Mira turned the page and found that the remainder was blank. The gift was not a map, a contract, or a judgment.

It was an invitation to keep working. She closed the notebook and watched the people crossing the bridge. The stone did not care which government had authorized it.

It held them because the builders had done their work well and because other people would return to repair it when it began to crack.

A little boy ran past holding two maps, one drawn by his mother and one by his grandfather. Their coastlines did not match. He asked which was the true one. Mira told him to look at the sea and make a third.

The End: THE SOLSTICE COVENANT.
